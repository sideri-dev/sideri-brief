Teenage Engineering says it's done making synthesizers as a business — hardware "isn't the business" anymore.

Founder Jesper Kouthoofd told Highsnobiety the company's products no longer matter financially on their own: "we shouldn't make profits out of the products," "the real value lies in the company as a whole." MusicTech independently confirms he's ceasing synth production immediately, with some future projects not shipping for five to ten years. He reframes the flagship OP-1 synthesizer as "therapy," not "a productive tool," estimating that 99% of buyers never actually use it to make music. It's a sharp reversal for a company whose entire reputation was built on beautifully made instrument hardware, and worth watching for anyone designing hardware in that same lineage.

A new memory architecture lets a system answer questions about 3D objects without replaying the original video.

A paper posted to arXiv, "Never Look Back: Understanding Persistence in 3D Object Memory from Egocentric Videos," builds a lasting record of objects seen in first-person video that can answer spatial questions later without re-accessing the footage itself. It lifts accuracy on the HD-EPIC benchmark from 29.7% to 42.6%, improves a second benchmark from 33.8% to 38.5%, and localizes objects from the Ego4D dataset to a median error of 0.99 meters. It's a concrete technical candidate for any on-device camera system trying to answer "where did I put that" from compressed memory rather than scrubbing back through raw footage.

Researchers built a conversational agent that answers questions about your own camera roll — with a telling recommendation slip.

"Personal AI Agent for Camera Roll VQA" introduces a dataset of 50 users, 31,476 images and 2,500 question-answer pairs, plus an agent with hierarchical memory for navigating someone's own photo archive — the closest public benchmark yet to retrieving from a person's own accumulated history rather than a generic photo dataset. Worth noting: one of the paper's own example queries asks the agent to "recommend some dishes I have never eaten before" — a recommendation-style behavior that sits oddly next to any design philosophy built around a retrieval system that only answers what's asked, never ranks or suggests on its own.

A new benchmark shows AI models are still far worse than humans at reasoning across events in first-person video.

EMBER-Bench tests 16 models on 189 household tasks and 699 question-answer pairs requiring causal reasoning across separate events in long first-person video. The best model scores 61.2% accuracy against a human average of 98.3% — a wide gap on exactly the "what happened earlier that explains what's happening now" reasoning that any camera system trying to understand a person's own process over time would depend on.

Eight of Japan's biggest manufacturers are showing unreleased in-house prototypes at a Tokyo gallery, October 30 to November 8.

AXIS Gallery in Roppongi stages "Industrial Romanticism 2" with in-house design teams from Sharp, Sony Group, TOTO, Panasonic, Fujifilm, Pola, Mitsubishi Electric and Yamaha, each showing a prototype built around the exhibition's theme. Yamaha's own release confirms this is its second appearance (after a first edition in 2025) and previews a wooden-box piano. It's a rare public look at how Japan's largest corporate design labs stage speculative, unreleased hardware for a general audience without a permanent retail footprint.

Yesterday's deep-dive

An earlier deep-dive corrected an industrial research group's hand-capture rig: it isn't a hand-mounted passive-capture wearable, but a handheld, actively-operated 3D-printed gripper run by 179 paid operators across 22 desks, with the computer sitting on a desk rather than worn. The open-hardware release is genuinely detailed at the mechanical level — dozens of 3D-printable parts and a royalty-free manufacturing grant — but closed exactly where capture happens: the hand-tracking layer is an unpublished app, and the underlying dataset is access-gated. Its cameras run at a modest resolution with depth sensing turned off to manage bandwidth — a well-funded industrial team hitting the same data-bandwidth wall a much smaller system would. The wider field it sits in has converged on wide-field-of-view cameras, which matches what a system with no fixed aim needs, but its most developed feature is rating performance and flagging mistakes — the opposite of a system designed only to answer, never rank or judge.
