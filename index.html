A new benchmark finds that, for on-device personal-memory assistants, the retrieval backend matters far more than the size of the model reading it.

MemArena v2, accepted at NeurIPS 2026, tested 50 simulated agents over 15 days of ego-centric dialogue and found that switching the memory-retrieval backend lifted accuracy by up to 22 percentage points, while scaling the reader model up gained at most 4.4 points — the backend, not the model, is where accuracy in this category actually lives. It also surfaced a sharper problem: an "Oracle" retrieval mode leaks protected information outright, while the permission-aware alternatives swing the other way and fail to surface a fact the agent is actually allowed to see. On a Spark GB10 edge node the search step itself adds only 8–87 milliseconds depending on backend, cheap enough that this is purely an architecture choice, not a speed tradeoff. It bears directly on how we think about retrieval architecture and the resource budget for the edge hardware we're prototyping on — enough that we're digging into it further.

A Senegalese weaver's forty-year lesson: modernizing the loom is how you keep a hand technique alive, not how you lose it.

Designer Aissa Dione widened Senegal's traditional 15–20cm looms to 90cm to weave at furniture scale, later bought secondhand 1970s–80s mechanical looms from Senegal and Lyon to cut costs, and had to re-source her thread entirely after a supplier secretly swapped to weaker Chinese thread that kept snapping mid-weave. Every one of her looms, however large or however mechanized, is still worked by two people by hand — modernization changed the tools, not the technique. It's a clean real-world case for treating visible, legible craft and a willingness to modernize production as complements rather than opposites — part of how a premium hand-made category survives at all.

Singapore's National Gallery is building a Nanyang artist retrospective on process material, not just finished paintings.

"Crossings," running through October 2027, is the first solo show on painter Lai Foong Moi since the 1960s, and the gallery's own framing leans on photographs and exhibition catalogues alongside the paintings as the route to "new insights into her artistic development." It's a concrete Southeast Asian instance of a pattern worth watching: serious institutions increasingly treat an artist's process record, not just the finished output, as collection-worthy in its own right.

Yesterday's deep dive

Yesterday we highlighted a Raspberry Pi blog post about retrofitting its UK headquarters' building-management system onto Raspberry Pi hardware at a fraction of a proprietary quote — read at the time as multi-year unattended-monitoring evidence for the board family our own hardware is built on. A closer look found that framing doesn't survive the source: the post gives the new system no runtime at all (nine months is the outer bound, not "multi-year"), the headline cost ratio compares an unscoped hypothetical against a real nearby UK quote that puts the true ratio closer to 3:1 than 19:1, and the building-management half actually ships on an older Raspberry Pi 3, not a 4 or 5. The more durable finding sits underneath: the vendor behind it has shipped Raspberry-Pi-based building controllers for six-plus years and documents zero watchdog, uptime, or SD-card wear handling — its update path is literally re-flashing the card — meaning the reliability bar we hold our own prototype to is already ahead of this niche's shipped state of the art, not behind it. A couple of factual corrections this surfaced are being weighed before anything is finalized.
