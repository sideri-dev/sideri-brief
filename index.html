Today

TrendForce is forecasting DRAM prices up 10–15% and NAND flash up 15–20% quarter-on-quarter in Q4 2026, both driven by AI and server demand.

This is the first forecast we've seen specifically on NAND flash rather than DRAM alone, confirmed independently by a German outlet and a Chinese outlet both relaying the same report. It's the component class underneath local-first storage designs, including the Raspberry Pi 5 platform we're building on, and it says the current memory squeeze keeps tightening into Q4 rather than easing.

A new research paper, ReaLMem, builds the first benchmark from real multi-year personal photo archives, paired with a module (ChronoProfiler) that weights and resolves conflicts in what it finds.

ChronoProfiler computes "temporal stability scores" for a person's attributes and uses them to decide which of several conflicting preferences to trust over time. That's functionally a ranking and salience layer sitting on top of exactly the kind of multi-year personal image archive an infinite-canvas retrieval system would hold — interesting as the fourth paper in a row to make this move, worth watching as a pattern.

FIND – Design Fair Asia is relocating from Singapore to Bangkok for its fifth edition, running November 26–28 at the Queen Sirikit National Convention Center.

After four editions in Singapore, a major regional design and furniture fair is moving its whole operation to Bangkok, citing the city's own hotel-construction pipeline (16,000-plus rooms underway) as part of the case. For a market we're already prioritizing, a fair of this size choosing Bangkok over Singapore is a concrete signal, and it fits well with a pop-up event presence rather than a permanent showroom.

Designer Yinka Ilori has published "Chairman," a monograph and exhibition of his own chair designs from 2006 to 2023, each one tied to a specific relationship in his life — shown in the community center on the estate where he grew up, not a gallery.

"All these chairs, for me, are people," he says; "these chairs are all fragile and delicate stories." It's a working designer treating seventeen years of his own objects as a maker-owned, autobiographical record, choosing to show it in the place that shaped it rather than a conventional retail venue.

Artist Dima Srouji's new commission, "Four Moons From Home," is built from months of her own field photography and archival early-20th-century wildflower books, carved into four stone windows.

"Jerusalem stone is not just a material," she says, "it is a carrier of place, of memory, of geology specific to that land." The piece leans more on her broader practice — elsewhere she's recreated museum-held archaeological vessels in glass — than it does standing entirely on its own, but it's a second instance this month of a research process feeding directly into a finished craft object.

Yesterday's deep dive

A closer look at yesterday's item on a planned AI-powered pendant found most of its load-bearing claims didn't hold up: the "memo" story traces to one reporter reading one document, not multiple corroborating sources, and the financial figures attached to it were a full quarter stale — the company in question reported its actual Q3 2026 results the same day the follow-up ran, showing its wearables division's revenue falling and its loss narrowing, with spend being reallocated toward AI generally. Neither the pendant nor a related business-subscription plan has shipped or been reported on since early June. The one finding that is genuinely new: the company now ships a first-party open-source SDK whose supported-board list explicitly includes the Raspberry Pi boards this build already runs on — but its license caps it at 50 devices for personal, non-commercial use and bars embedding it in anything sold for payment. Net effect: the "platform threat" framing in the original item was largely backwards — that wearables push looks like it's retreating, not expanding — but the SDK licensing detail is a genuinely new constraint worth tracking.
