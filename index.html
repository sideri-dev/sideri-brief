Today

A Tokyo studio turned a manufacturer's own shipping defect into the product's selling point — and the manufacturer is on record saying so.

Design studio Nature Architects takes Toray's PICASUS® optical film and deliberately folds, heats, compresses and twists it, turning the exact wrinkle and tube-rolling marks that Toray's own quality-assurance team treats as an unforgivable shipping flaw into the finished piece's visible feature. Toray's own quality lead calls the studio's reframing — essentially, "this is where the beauty is" — a genuine surprise after twenty years of never trying it themselves. It's a precedent at the material level for the idea that visible evidence of a making process can be the premium, rather than a defect to hide, and the show (Tokyo International Forum, opens November 26) is close enough that a deeper look felt worth running today.

A new research benchmark says no current AI model can decide, in the moment, what a wearable camera should remember.

The ProactEgoMem benchmark tests models on three jobs any always-on capture device needs solved with nobody watching: catching transient detail before it's gone, tracking how objects change, and tracking where they move — and finds that even the strongest models fail to do this both promptly and accurately. It's exactly the decision an unattended capture pipeline has to make for itself, and sharpens an active debate over where retrieving a record ends and a system quietly choosing on a person's behalf begins.

A London exhibition exposes years of chairs a well-known designer built privately — and never let near his own studio's client work.

Yinka Ilori's new show and monograph, "Chairman," gathers chairs he made for himself and kept deliberately apart on principle: "these are fragile and delicate stories, and I can't take someone's personal story and make it a studio project." The two outlets covering it disagree on how many years that private practice spans — seventeen by one account, "about a decade" by the other — both are worth holding rather than picking one. What matters past the discrepancy: years of unshown personal work, made for no one but the maker, only now being brought into public view.

A Shenzhen wearable-AI maker just raised a second big funding round — selling a philosophy that runs opposite to our own.

Looki, maker of a camera-based ambient-capture wearable, closed a several-hundred-million-yuan round led by Yuansheng Capital, with existing backers piling in again. The company's own description of what it's building — a "physical-world AI recommendation engine" — is close to a direct inversion of the idea that a personal record should answer a question, never rank or suggest one. Worth tracking as the closest thing to a direct competitor on hardware form, moving fast in the opposite philosophical direction.

Yesterday's deep-dive

Yesterday's closer look settled a dispute over a flagship camera-brand store that opened in Tokyo's Omotesando district this week: it's run by the brand's own distributor, not the brand itself, despite one outlet's headline claiming otherwise. The sharper finding is structural — the brand had already run a smaller, time-limited pop-up presence in Japan first, decided that wasn't enough, and then had its distributor sign the permanent lease rather than opening a store itself; a brand-run version of this same kind of store has already failed twice at this exact address. Priced against local rent benchmarks, a month of short-term pop-up presence costs less than a single month's rent at a comparable space, and the best available research on brand-run retail shows a company's own online sales falling by over a fifth after this kind of store opens. On balance, the evidence argues against ever opening a storefront of this kind — with one narrow complication the piece records rather than resolves.
