Raspberry Pi's firmware permanently locks RAM to its factory configuration — and it now provably reaches the standard Pi 5, not just a specialized Compute Module

A Raspberry Pi engineer confirmed it plainly on the company's own forum: devices are locked to their original RAM size "to remove the commercial incentive" for swapping in cheaper chips, and switching parts of the same size may not even work. The complaint that surfaced it was a Compute Module 5 case, but independent testing by hardware reviewer Jeff Geerling closes the gap to the ordinary Pi 5 directly — he traced the same firmware change to late 2024 and found no workaround short of running two-year-old firmware.

This sits on the board we're building on, and it rules out board-level RAM repair or upgrade as an option — plus it's a live signal that a resold or relabeled unit with mismatched RAM attributes simply won't boot.

A new foundation model (MINT) predicts hand visibility frame-by-frame from ordinary RGB video — the closest match yet to an RGB camera's sensor type

Trained on ego-centric footage, it jointly tracks camera trajectory and per-frame hand observability from a single video representation, hitting 94.5% frame accuracy and a 3.67x speedup over the offline pipeline used to label its own training data; model, code and a 1,021-hour dataset are all released.

It's a genuinely different sensor path from most recent hand-tracking work, which assumes an event camera rather than a standard RGB one like our Camera Module 3 — architecture evidence toward an on-device trigger for "when to notice hands," not yet a deployable one (the speedup is against an offline labeling process, not a real-time number on small hardware).

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04958

HP and Lenovo both raised prices in mid-September, and the reason is the same memory squeeze already hitting Raspberry Pi

HP lifted client-device prices 10-15%, Lenovo about 6% on average (4-10% by configuration), both citing memory and storage supply constrained by AI-datacenter demand, with guidance pointing to volatility "through the end of 2026 and into 2027." Independent industry coverage backs the underlying mechanism — DRAM contract prices rose 13-18% quarter-on-quarter in Q3, driven by the same AI-server demand.

Two PC-scale vendors absorbing double-digit cost increases, with no relief signaled until well into next year, is a strong argument that single-board-computer sourcing exposure right now is a structural cost shift, not a temporary spike to wait out.

Capture One raised prices 6% across its entire lineup, and said so plainly rather than hiding it in a relaunch

The photo-editing software's Pro, All-in-One and Studio tiers all went up — monthly, annual and perpetual licenses alike — starting in June, with the company's own line: "costs more now than it did a year ago... we're continuing to invest in the platform."

It's a clean, named precedent for how a premium creative tool talks about a cost-driven price increase to its own user base without discounting the brand.

A Japanese stationery brand's ¥770 sketchbook marks the very bottom of a price ladder worth watching

MUCU's sketchbook uses two pieces of recycled paperboard for covers, a canvas spine, and 60 pages of the recycled paper Japanese manga is printed on — sold in Japan only, since "tariffs and shipping would eat up any cost savings" for export.

It's thin on its own, but it's a specific, dated data point at the floor of the Japanese creator-stationery price range — a marker, not a serious competitor.

Yesterday's deep-dive

An earlier deep-dive corrected an item from the day before: the paper's headline storage-reduction number turns out to measure a transient, build-time training buffer, not the archive index actually served — the served centroids ship as plain float32 — so it doesn't answer whether a full personal capture archive's search index fits on a small on-device computer. The real constraint is far bigger than anything this paper touches: raw capture data dwarfs the index by roughly 900:1.

On the upside, the digging surfaced a live open-source candidate for on-device vector search worth tracking — SQLite Vec1, a dependency-free single-file library with ARM NEON support and active commits as recently as September 25 — against a comparable but dormant alternative that hasn't shipped a stable release in over four months. One more correction worth noting: the paper is properly a companion to, not the same work as, the "SuperKMeans" project named in the original item.
