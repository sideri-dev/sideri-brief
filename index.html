Today

Raspberry Pi's own UK headquarters now runs its building-management system on Raspberry Pi 4 and 5 hardware, replacing a dead 17-year-old proprietary controller at a fraction of the cost.

The old Trend 963 interface failed earlier this year; a like-for-like replacement quote came in at £150,000. Raspberry Pi built its own open BACnet system instead — Pi 4 and 5 boards running Home Assistant, with Emporia clamp sensors — for £7,800, about one-nineteenth of the quote. It's vendor-published and single-source, but it's real-world, continuous-duty evidence of exactly the kind of thing worth watching on this board family: it running unattended as a control system, not a hobby project.

reMarkable launched Paper Pure, a $399 black-and-white e-paper tablet, and simply discontinued the reMarkable 2 to make room for it — color and frontlight stay exclusive to the pricier Pro line.

At $399, with a $449 Marker Plus/Sleeve Folio bundle, Paper Pure gets the same writing surface as the Pro model but none of its premium display features, and it replaces the old entry-level device outright rather than sitting beside it. It's a clean precedent for widening a premium instrument's reach without touching the flagship's price or positioning: retire the old entry point instead of discounting the one people actually want.

Industrial design studio Layer, working with Canadian manufacturer Corelam, developed a way to corrugate 3mm plywood under pressure lamination for a real strength-to-weight gain, shown first in a table called "Ripple."

It's an internal studio research project, not a commissioned product. The strength-to-weight gain comes from the corrugation geometry itself rather than added material — a lightweight-but-strong fabrication technique worth noting for anyone thinking about how a small enclosure might be built differently. It's the thinnest item on today's list, but it names a real process rather than a vague trend.

Yesterday's deep-dive

An earlier deep-dive tested DESIGNART Tokyo, the city's annual design festival, against the idea of presenting hardware at pop-up events rather than in a retail showroom — and the venue passed as a presentation and press stage while failing as a sales channel: festival-wide sales per exhibitor run roughly ¥118,000–¥197,000, below even the cheapest paid package once space, build and staffing are added, and the organizer's own report says exhibitors lean on their own e-commerce instead. The flagship structure, "AUTOBAHN," a 2,000-square-metre temporary build on a decommissioned stretch of elevated expressway, turns out to be curator-invited only, with no price, no application and no path in for this year, and nothing about next year's edition is published at all. The one close precedent found inside it was a single exhibitor, a former Suzuki industrial designer showing an audio-interface piece, tagged by the organizer as both a product and for sale.

This week

Four separate investigations this week — into a funding story, a data-sale story, a synth company's exit, and Raspberry Pi's own update service — converged on one finding: the promise that a personal record stays owned by its maker is currently backed up at the file layer only, not at whether a device can still boot, update or decrypt that file once a vendor's key or server is gone. Raspberry Pi's own supported production path burns an irreversible security key into the Pi 5 at manufacture with no recovery route, and its free remote-access service has already had its limits cut twice with no stated floor. Nothing here is settled — it's a hardware-security choice that has to be made at first manufacture, not patched in afterward.

A cluster of ambient-capture launches this week — Ambi, Amazon's Bee, and the smart ring maker Natura — got described as occupying the same space as always-on personal-capture devices generally, but a closer look found no funded company actually building a passive process-archive a maker owns outright; those products are conversation- and meeting-capture tools, cloud-metered or subscription-priced, which is a different category. Research into Singapore's privacy law found the specific case of someone capturing their own working process, sensing continuously but recording only selected moments, addressed by no existing rule; Japan's rules are the closest analogue, and they treat a visibly camera-shaped object as part of compliance. Both threads point at the same open design question: a clear signal that a device is sensing, which nobody building in this space has shipped yet.

Three companies — the personal-AI hardware startup Ghost, Google (via its MediaPipe toolkit), and Raspberry Pi — each published a headline this week that their own fine print contradicts: Ghost's "no data ever leaves your home" sits beside a managed cloud gateway for several features; MediaPipe's "single-digit millisecond" on-device search holds only up to roughly 2,600 stored records before slowing down; and Raspberry Pi's only published noise figure for its own cooling fan is an unsourced number from a 2023 blog post, never an actual specification. Each claim is one that any maker of similar hardware would want to make honestly — which means stating the real operating point (how many records, how loud under what conditions, which data actually stays local) rather than repeating the headline as written.
