Today

A Kobe demolition contract spells out, in procurement language, exactly the capture-before-destruction record this research is tracking.

Kobe City's own requirements for demolishing the Important Cultural Property "Old Hunter House" mandate a full component ledger and reusable-material record across the whole demolition, so the building could be rebuilt later, even though it's coming down for seismic reasons. It's a government buyer stating, in writing, exactly what a "capture before loss" record must contain, as a paid deliverable — a rare primary look at what heritage-capture work actually requires and who pays for it.

A Japanese court cleared the way to demolish one of Kenzo Tange's buildings — and the only thing offered in its place is a record.

A Takamatsu court rejected an injunction against demolishing Tange's former Kagawa prefectural gymnasium, ruling that neighbors' view and living-environment interests aren't legally protected. The prefecture's governor, facing calls to preserve it, committed only to "proceeding carefully with record preservation" — not to saving the building, which comes down at a stated cost of ¥847 million. A named, architecturally significant building loses its legal shield, and record-keeping is the one concession anyone is offering.

A government archive just put a number on how much of Japan's modern-architecture canon has already vanished: over 40 buildings.

Japan's Cultural Affairs Agency, through its National Archives of Modern Architecture, is marking its DOCOMOMO Japan list passing 300 selected buildings with an exhibition whose own text states that more than 40 of those tracked buildings are already lost. It's a government institution quantifying, in public, the exact loss-before-documentation gap that makes architectural archiving valuable in the first place.

A 190-year-old registered heritage building in Kumamoto collapsed this month, three months after an earthquake condemned it, while the town was still deciding what to do.

The building, in the town of Hikawa, was judged a total loss after July's magnitude-7 earthquake but stood leaning through months of municipal deliberation before it finally came down on October 16, with no record of it known to have existed beforehand. A blunt illustration of a timing problem: the decision process can outlast the building, but not by much, and nothing guarantees anyone captures it in between.

NVIDIA released a vision encoder that claims to match a much bigger, more restrictively licensed model — at a fraction of the size, under an open license.

C-RADIOv4, distilled from three strong teacher models (SigLIP2, DINOv3 and SAM3) and shipped in two sizes — 412 million and 631 million parameters — is, by NVIDIA's own paper, "competitive with DINOv3 on dense tasks at a fraction of the parameters," landing close to DINOv3-7B's benchmark scores at roughly a tenth of the parameter count, under a stated permissive license. DINOv3 remains the strongest encoder seen so far for matching artwork and in-progress creative work pixel-for-pixel with no labels, but it ships under a restrictive, gated license from Meta — C-RADIOv4 looks like the candidate that could close that gap under an open one.

This week

The ambient-capture wearable class (devices that record passively, worn on the body) had its core premise refuted at the vendors' own pages this week: Plaud's own Japanese help page shows a permanent free tier with no subscription required; Natura's pages never mention a price or a subscription anywhere; of the three devices in the class (Plaud, Amazon's Bee, and Natura), only Bee actually carries a subscription, and even that one started after the comparison was first drawn. The more durable contrast isn't pricing at all — it's custody: every device in this class is a thin client to a metered cloud service, while this project's own approach keeps the record local and whole.

Two unrelated research threads converged on the same finding about Japan's radio-equipment certification: the certification mark can pass automatically by statute to a finished product built on an already-certified Raspberry Pi board, with no fee or extra permission needed from Raspberry Pi itself, and the relevant Japanese certificates for the Raspberry Pi 5 and Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W are already public and downloadable. Japanese wearable maker Memoket's own certification history for its Gem device independently confirmed that pursuing a certification directly, rather than inheriting one, takes about ten weeks end to end via mutual recognition. What the statute does not cover is everything built around the board itself — the enclosure, internal cabling, the camera ribbon cable, and the charging adapter — which is where certification attention now belongs.

The Raspberry Pi board family's memory-price squeeze continued this week: an $80–$210 price jump landed on exactly the 16GB memory configuration, the one tier with no second supplier, while other configurations held steady. On the Japanese retail shelf, the same Raspberry Pi 5 now sits at two different prices across two different sellers — Switch Science and Akizuki — overnight, a live example of the kind of stock-and-price signal this research is trying to decide whether to track as its own category.
