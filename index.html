Today

Raspberry Pi's own blog shows a 14-megabyte on-device language model triggering a camera capture in about 76 milliseconds, running CPU-only on a Raspberry Pi 5.

The model was already known to run locally with no GPU; what's new is that Raspberry Pi demonstrated it calling a declared "take a photo" function through the board's own camera command-line tool, timed at 76 milliseconds alongside four other actions (toggling an LED, reading temperature, saving a note, and a no-op). It's a named vendor demonstration of structured, declared camera-triggering on this exact board, which is useful evidence for how a tiny on-device model could decide, by itself, when to capture a photo. No other outlet has covered this post.

A robotics paper shows a hand-tracking pipeline's real-world success collapsing to 9.3% once objects start blocking the camera's view.

In grocery-store and pharmacy pick-and-place tests, a pipeline built on Google's MediaPipe Hands saw its task success fall to 9.3% once surrounding objects occluded the hand; swapping in a different hand detector, WiLoR, improved detection rate by 8% over MediaPipe. It's a robotics-teleoperation study rather than a personal-capture one, but it's a concrete, quantified occlusion failure for a widely used hand-tracking library, with a quantified alternative.

A Nagoya museum opened an exhibition built entirely from one aristocrat's century-old travel photographs.

Tokugawa Yoshichika's own photographs — streets, buildings, artworks, the people he met on his travels — go on display today under the museum's own framing: "see, capture, collect, bring home." It's a clean historical example of passive personal capture becoming, unedited, the artifact a public institution exhibits a century later — confirmed independently on both the arts press and the museum's own page.

An artist's mobile tea-room truck deliberately keeps every scratch and trace of its past owners rather than restoring them away.

Riko Takahashi's installation treats the vehicle's accumulated history as the point — its meaning, in the artist's own words, "updates through accumulated encounters and dialogue with people" rather than being fixed at the moment it was made. It's a working artist independently arriving at a doctrine worth naming plainly: a record that accrues meaning over time instead of being pruned, edited, or ranked.

Yesterday's deep-dive

An earlier deep-dive read a widely-cited academic framework for turning long first-person workplace video into structured event records, and found it to be an eight-page workshop abstract with no results section at all, whose own description of its central data structure disagrees with itself in three separate places. The sharpest finding: the system's retrieval provably never ranks its results, but the same two researchers' published industrial system, built on the same dataset, explicitly "recommends the next valid action" instead. On compute, one of its two vision encoders turned out to be impractical on small hardware — a single clip takes over six seconds on a 96-core workstation against under 50 milliseconds on a GPU — and nobody has ever actually run either encoder on board-class hardware; every such number published so far is arithmetic, not a measurement.
