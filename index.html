Apple shipped its answer to image provenance, and it's built the opposite way from an on-device approach.

Reference Image on the iPhone 18 Pro and Pro Max cryptographically signs raw sensor data immediately at capture, round-trips through Apple's Private Cloud Compute, and was viewable only on Apple's own platforms at launch — confirmed independently by Apple's own security blog and IEEE Spectrum. It's a real, shipping, sign-at-sensor system, and it's cloud-dependent and platform-locked in exactly the ways an on-device, no-cloud-dependency approach to image authenticity isn't. One outside expert's read: it doesn't solve the underlying problem, but it may push other manufacturers to take image authenticity seriously.

Yesterday's deep-dive

A deep-dive into Japan's new biometric-privacy amendment found the trigger story overstated on both load-bearing points: it's enacted law, not a bill still moving through parliament, but none of its biometric provisions are actually in force yet — they wait on a cabinet-order date with a legal outer limit years out, so nothing in it binds a device shipping soon. Its real subject is converted facial-feature codes, not photographs, so a device that captures frames without computing a face-authentication template doesn't enter the new regime at all. What already applies, and matters more, is an existing duty that a camera be visibly recognizable as a camera, a rule that a date-searchable-but-not-person-searchable archive doesn't count as a regulated "personal database," and a 2023 advisory that puts the compliance burden on the user, asking the maker only to document capture, storage, review and erasure in the manual. Left open on purpose: whether a non-authentication retrieval embedding counts as identifying data at all, and whether a person-searchable index would change that answer.
