Today

Raspberry Pi says it will handle a customer's country-by-country compliance approvals itself, backed by its own RF test chamber.

A new post from Raspberry Pi names "Global Market Access" as a formal service: its lead, Alex Branton, says it "handles country-specific approvals on your behalf" and can "get them for you" when entering a new market. Behind it sits an in-house 3-metre RF anechoic chamber plus UKAS-accredited labs, and separate in-house shock/vibration, thermal, reliability, UV-weathering and XRF testing, with "all our certificates, declarations of conformity, and test reports" published on the company's own portal. No specific country, standard or board is named — this describes the mechanism, not a claim about any one product. Worth reading closely for a prototype built on the Raspberry Pi 5, with the Pi Zero 2 W as a fallback board: it's the first time the vendor has described, in its own words, an actual compliance-outsourcing offer rather than just a components-are-certified claim.

Realbotix is taking $199 reservations on companion robots, with a $24,950 vision upgrade on top of $12,000 to $60,000 hardware.

Realbotix, based in Las Vegas, opened reservations on four humanoid companion robots — Aria, Mila, Dave and Amar — with tabletop units at $11,950 and full-body versions at $59,950, deliveries planned for the second half of 2027. Vision and eye-tracking is a separate $24,950 add-on called "Vinci," and actual conversation access is subscription-gated starting at $19.99 a month. One correction worth flagging: Core77's own article lists the reservation deposit as $200, but at least four other outlets reading the same launch materials all say $199 — the more reliable figure. Either way, it's about as extreme as the hardware-plus-subscription model gets: the robot's basic perception, seeing you, sits behind its own five-figure paywall on top of the device price.

Cal AI's 19-year-old co-founder raised $10M for a $179 screenless wristband shipping in December.

Zach Yadegari, co-founder of the calorie-tracking app Cal AI, raised $10M — led by Vine Ventures, with Cory Levy's Z Fellows and Collective Global participating — for a new startup called Persona. The product is a $179 wearable band that activates on a button press or a wrist-flick rather than listening continuously, with units expected in December. It's a different mechanic from passive always-on capture — a voice assistant triggered by explicit action — but it's a fresh, funded low-price anchor in the broader AI-wearable-hardware category.

A London designer turned the inhaler case into the whole product.

Matt Conlon, self-taught in CAD through YouTube tutorials, built "Hale Classic" — a roughly 1mm aluminium case with custom magnets that snaps onto a standard asthma inhaler, offered in gold, silver and brushed grey. He worked with a 3D modeller and CNC manufacturers to bring it to production. His own framing: "It works perfectly – it's people's relationship with the inhaler that's the problem," and he wanted it to "feel like an actual inhaler, not a bulky accessory that feels tight in your jean pocket." It's a clean example of turning a stigmatized, overlooked medical object into a premium design object purely through material and finish.

Yesterday's deep-dive

A closer look at Memoket Gem, the Japanese AI wearable recorder featured a day earlier, found its no-subscription pitch isn't the clean differentiator it first looked like: Plaud, the category's price leader, already offers an identical free 300-minutes-a-month tier with no subscription required, and Memoket's ¥31,980 price sits on top of an existing ¥24,990–30,800 no-subscription price ladder, not outside it. Its Japanese storefront displayed a lower price, ¥27,183, but that turned out to be a leaked development-preview mock-up; the store's own backend data confirms ¥31,980 as the real, transacting price. The one genuinely new fact: the device's Japanese radio certification was granted through a Spanish mutual-recognition body off a Shenzhen lab's test report in about ten weeks, never touching a Japanese certification body, and the certificate number appears nowhere in Memoket's own materials. Its "KITE" retrieval engine also splits in two: the published research behind it is strictly query-driven, but the shipped product sells proactive digests and automatic cross-recording surfacing as headline features.
