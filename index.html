Tokyo is running a live AI-sorting recycling box for small batteries and electronics, right at the Metropolitan Government Building.

The bin, built on Polish recognition tech called Weecle and deployed by a local operator, takes phones, power banks, cameras, earbuds and similar small devices within a size limit, and rewards depositors with city loyalty points. It's a single-source find so far, but it's a real, currently-running instance of exactly the kind of small-device collection infrastructure our own no-loose-battery, user-replaceable-cell design choices are built to work with in Japan.

MIT built a lab-stage chip that does thermal imaging and gas detection on the same sensor array.

A 6-by-6 pixel array made from a phase-change material gives independent control over mid-infrared light at each pixel, and the researchers say the architecture should scale to millions of pixels without the current-leakage problems that usually block that. It's a research demo, not a sourceable part, but it sits squarely on the same sensing axis we're building on — thermal plus gas detection, currently as two separate chips — so it's worth watching as the class matures.

The Supreme x Analogue Pocket collaboration finally has real pricing, and it's a clean case study in scarcity economics.

The gold colorway sold for $588, the red for $498 — both well over double the standard Pocket's $239.99 — and every color sold out within seconds of the drop going live. It's a useful comparison for a flat-price, no-drop instrument: scarcity mechanics, not spec sheets or bill-of-materials, are what set this kind of premium, and the same drop format could plausibly work through pop-up events rather than an ongoing storefront.

Yesterday's deep-dive

A closer read of Texas's new right-to-repair law found it weaker than the headline suggests, and gated somewhere the original coverage missed. The law only reaches equipment "sold to a consumer... for personal, family, or household purposes" — a test that sits upstream of every exemption in the statute, and the text never says whether that's judged per sale or per product line, so a device sold mainly to professionals may fall outside the law entirely, unresolved either way. Of four US state repair statutes read in full, Texas turns out to be the weakest: it explicitly allows manufacturers to require matched parts, has no path for a manufacturer's own in-house repair work to count as compliance, and sets no small-manufacturer exemption anywhere. Minnesota's 60-day response clock, California's seven-year parts tail and cost floor, and Oregon's ban on parts-matching are the provisions that would actually bind a future product — not Texas's. None of this changes anything today, but it corrects the record on what the law actually requires.
