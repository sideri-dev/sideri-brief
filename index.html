GoPro's new interchangeable-lens camera holds $100 back from anyone who isn't already a subscriber.

The MISSION 1 PRO ILS, GoPro's first Micro Four Thirds camera (50MP sensor, 8K60 video), opened for pre-order on August 26 at $699.99 — or $599.99 "for existing GoPro subscribers," full price for everyone else. It's a sharp real-world example of a hardware maker choosing to make its price legible only through an ongoing subscription relationship, a direct contrast to building a hardware business around a single upfront price with no subscription attached. Worth following further, given GoPro is simultaneously mid-sale-process with collapsing quarterly shipments — an open question whether lock-in pricing like this is propping the business up or accelerating its trouble.

Fellow's founder was rejected by investors 73 times before anyone wrote a check — then spent a decade refusing real institutional money.

Jake Miller got his first $250,000 from an angel investor he met at a Starbucks in Redwood City; he didn't raise a proper institutional round until 2022, closing $30 million. His own words: "If someone would have given me $10 million ten years ago, I would have spent all that money on some product that the world didn't need." The Espresso Series 1 that eventually emerged from that discipline cost more than $6 million and three years of work, with about 25 people on it — a rare concrete cost-and-headcount comparable for a single premium-hardware product built this way.

Vermicular made its first-ever appearance at a European trade fair, explicitly framed as market entry.

The Nagoya cast-iron cookware maker exhibited at Ambiente 2026 in Frankfurt, describing the booth as part of an initiative aimed at "full-scale entry into the European market, birthplace of enameled cast-iron cookware," and was separately selected for the fair's curated trends showcase. It's a cheap, low-commitment way to test a new geography before committing to a distributor or local entity there — a template worth watching for anyone weighing a similar export move. Reported so far by a single source, the original release.

Nothing's budget sub-brand CMF now sells earbuds for $24.99 in the US, with a same-spec India launch on its own separate date and price.

The Buds Neo pack 52 hours of combined battery life, 35dB active noise cancellation, and a 12.4mm driver into a $25 shell shipping via Amazon; India gets the identical hardware through Flipkart, Croma and other retail chains at ₹1,999 on a different calendar. It shows Nothing's transparent-hardware design language stretched all the way down to a $25 price floor through a separate brand — a counter-case to any single-SKU, no-budget-tier strategy.

Yesterday's deep-dive

A "showroom" story about Bambu Lab's Tokyo presence turned out smaller than first reported: a 14-person distributor gave walk-in hours and a till to an appointment-only showroom it had already been quietly running at the same address — not a new site, and weaker counter-evidence to a no-showroom retail strategy than it first appeared. More telling is what the original coverage missed: eleven days earlier, Bambu Lab placed a printer into a real curated retail slot with its own press release, meaning the brand paid for the higher-tier retail placement rather than substituting a distributor showroom for it. The original "two independent sources" behind the story turned out to be rewrites of the same distributor press release. What the story does open, genuinely, is a distinct question about distributor-hosted retail, separate from the direct-showroom question it first appeared to raise.
