Today

Raspberry Pi raised the price of its 16GB boards again — the 16GB Pi 5 jumps $80 to $385, the 16GB Compute Module 5 by $40.

Raspberry Pi's own post blames "a lack of alternative supply for 16GB DRAM, compounded by other ongoing component shortages," and spares the Pi 500+ despite it carrying the same 16GB. This is at least the second memory-driven price action on this exact board line inside a year, and the company's own closing line — "the current memory landscape is difficult but ultimately temporary" — is doing a lot of work for a maker that keeps raising the price of the same product. It's the board we're building our own prototype on, so each hike is a live, compounding cost signal rather than background noise.

Natura put real numbers behind its "Interface" ring for the first time — 6 to 12 days of battery life, 13 hours of continuous recording, a 100-minute full charge.

The company's own FAQ also gives the charging case 10 full recharges, sizes the ring from US 7 to 13 in stainless steel, and still withholds price and ship date until checkout. It's the clearest power-budget disclosure yet from the ambient-capture-wearable category we track by name (Plaud, Bee, Natura) — a direct comparable for the always-on reliability problem our own capture hardware has to solve, and a reminder that the whole category still runs on cheap, subsidized hardware plus a subscription, the inverse of a one-time, unsubsidized price.

Teenage Engineering shipped its first hardware outside the synth line, the EP-35 K.O. AMP — and nobody agrees on what it costs.

Japan's price is clean at roughly ¥69,850 for a 35W amp, but the US price is contested between two legitimate retail sources: MusicRadar says $349, Chicago Music Exchange's own site says $399. The launch lands days after founder Jesper Kouthoofd half-joked about dropping the synth division in favor of "the company as a whole" — making this a live test of whether Teenage Engineering's design-reference premium pricing travels outside the instrument category it built its reputation on.

Raspberry Pi formalized a "Powered by Raspberry Pi" compliance-accreditation program — and one accredited product is a commercial photo booth built on exactly the hardware pairing we use.

The program standardizes "compliance regulations and testing procedures," per Raspberry Pi's own channel, but names no specific radio or EMC standard it actually covers — a gap, not an answer, for whether a Raspberry Pi 5 and Camera Module 3 build like ours can lean on it for certification in Japan. Horace, the profiled booth, runs two Raspberry Pi High Quality Cameras and a Pi 5 streaming live 2K video — real, market-sold proof this exact camera-plus-board pairing already ships as a product.

Seiko is relaunching a quartz wall clock built around the design of the world's first household quartz clock, from 1968 — a piece the Japanese government itself has designated national heritage.

The original, Seiko's SPX-961, was registered in 2020 by the National Museum of Nature and Science as an "Essential Historical Materials for Science and Technology" item; the revival launches November 20 at ¥27,500. It's a clean, dated example of a Japanese manufacturer converting a heritage designation into the entire premium story for a launch — exactly the pattern of visible craft becoming the premium argument that's worth watching in the market we're building toward.

Yesterday's deep-dive

Yesterday's closer look mostly walked back the original story about Toray and Nature Architects' PICASUS® film exhibit: Toray's own patents describe the exact optical effect the show exploits and call it a defect, so the studio is reversing a correction Toray patented against, not discovering a new mechanism — and Toray's own commercial colour line made the same reversal roughly twenty years earlier. The practice is an established exhibition format in Japan with a traceable network of repeat studios, running back to 2001 at its cleanest dated precedent. The claim that no design theory names this method doesn't hold either — a 2011 book and a Politecnico di Milano research line already cover it. The sharpest finding for us sits elsewhere: the film is a metal-free, interference-based coating Toray states passes radio signals through it, which matters next to a board like the Raspberry Pi 5, whose only antenna sits on the circuit board itself, with no certified external-antenna path.
