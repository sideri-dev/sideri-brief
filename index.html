A security researcher shows a C2PA "valid" signature can cover zero bytes of the file it supposedly certifies

David Buchanan published a proof-of-concept that excludes an entire file from the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) signed hash — his manifest's exclusion field spans the whole file, leaving the signature covering an empty string instead of any actual content. He demonstrated it on a doctored lottery-ticket photo backdated to before its own draw, and neither the Content Authenticity Initiative's own hosted verifier nor the reference tool c2patool catches it. No fix has shipped as of this writing. The underlying mechanism first surfaced publicly on October 12, and Buchanan's own live demonstration followed on October 19; today's coverage, drawn from WorkOS's engineering blog and independently corroborated by PhotoWorkout and Zeli.app, confirms the flaw remains unpatched a month after Buchanan's original post.

Electron 45 ships with GPU-stability fixes for Chromium-based desktop apps

The new stable release of the Electron desktop-app framework bundles Chromium 156 and fixes a bug where the GPU process was being killed outright when WebGPU fell back to software rendering (SwiftShader) on Windows, plus a fix so GPU shaders compiled by Skia are cached between launches instead of recompiled every time. These are exactly the failure modes that matter for any GPU-heavy desktop app rendering large volumes of images, and the release had been expected since early October — this is the first look at what actually shipped in it.

Affinity Labs raised $2M in pre-seed funding — led by Fuel Ventures and Antler, with a roughly £500,000 non-dilutive UK Innovate UK grant covering about a third of the round — to co-found software products directly with individual creators, pitched as putting "an AI factory behind a creator's name." Yesterday's deep dive

An earlier deep-dive into a wearable AI company's Japan launch found the original framing didn't hold up: Japan is not excluded from that company's flagship product, as initially reported — the Japanese arm published its own version of the device the same evening, priced at ¥44,000 and waitlisted for release within the year, and several earlier reports needed correcting (a price that had been dropped as unsupported turned out to be accurate; one cited outlet was a press-release wire rather than original reporting). The real finding was narrower and more interesting: the product holds a genuine Japanese radio certification, but it covers only the Bluetooth earbuds, not the eSIM/cellular radio that the product's core function depends on — no telecom-level certification for that exists in the public record, and the Japan listing itself still reads "in preparation." On how fast this company is moving into Japan versus Southeast Asia, the investigation reports rather than concludes: it entered Japan with a full local company, tax registration and a thousand-plus retail-store footprint well before this specific product's own launch event, making it an outlier against two competitors in the same ambient-capture-wearable category, which have no comparable Japan presence at all.
