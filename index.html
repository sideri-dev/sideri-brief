Japan's telecom regulator has an unresolved fight on its hands: what happens when a product has no packaging to print a certification mark on.

A March 2026 briefing from Japan's Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, prepared for a cabinet-office startup working group, quotes manufacturer testimony that the current workaround — printing the country's radio-certification mark on packaging or in a manual when the device body can't carry it — breaks down entirely for a product shipped with neither. The same document records a consumer-harm account: people turned away at trade-in because the mark couldn't be confirmed. On-device digital display of the mark was legalized back in 2010, but the accompanying rule for software-updated devices was, as of this document, still under deliberation.

This bears directly on how we're planning to satisfy that requirement: showing the certification identity and firmware version on-screen from day one rather than burying it inside the device. The consumer-harm evidence in this document makes that look less like a nice-to-have and more like the direction the rule is heading anyway.

Espressif patched an HTTP request-smuggling bug in the exact firmware line we build on.

ESP-IDF v6.0.3, released September 2, fixes a Content-Length truncation issue in the built-in HTTP server that could enable request smuggling, and adds a new configuration option to cap request body size. The rest of the release is incremental — DMA color-conversion, camera RAW-from-memory support, minor driver additions.

Our firmware sits on this same v6.0 line, so the fix lands directly in what we ship, ahead of a planned move to the newer v6.1 line once it clears its own stability bar.

Espressif's newest development boards are real; the claim that they already run its machine-learning library is not vendor-confirmed.

Coverage of Espressif's ESP32-S31 Korvo/CoreBoard kits (dual-microphone array, 4.3-inch LCD, 3-megapixel camera) never mentions Espressif's own ESP-DL inference library. That claim traces instead to a community GitHub demo whose last commit predates the actual stable release of the ESP-IDF version it depends on — meaning it ran against a pre-release build — and Espressif's own ESP-DL repository doesn't list this chip among its officially supported targets yet.

We're tracking ESP-DL support on this chip as one of the signals for when it becomes viable for on-device machine-learning workloads, so it's worth knowing the current "it works" claim is a community result on preview software, not a vendor guarantee.

Balmuda's losses are shrinking, and its newest hit sells mostly overseas.

Balmuda's first-half 2026 results show revenue down 15.8% year over year to ¥4.365 billion, with a ¥314 million operating loss and a ¥331 million net loss — smaller than the ¥397 million net loss a year earlier. Its newest product, the Sailing Lantern, sells roughly 70% abroad.

We continue to watch Balmuda as a case study in how a premium design-led hardware brand behaves under sustained contraction, not as a direct comparable — and an export-heavy newest release is a data point worth keeping in view.

Shopify's automated duty-inclusive checkout now explicitly covers Japan.

Shopify Managed Markets bakes duty and import tax into a product's displayed price rather than surfacing it as a separate line at checkout, and Shopify guarantees the amount — it absorbs the difference if a customs authority charges more than calculated. Its published eligible-market list, previously unclear on this point, now explicitly names both Japan and Singapore alongside the UK, EU, Australia and others.

For a direct-to-consumer store shipping into these markets, this closes a real question about whether landed-cost checkout is even available where it would be needed.

Yesterday's deep-dive

A closer read of the EU's Cyber Resilience Act, prompted by the reporting-deadline item covered previously, found the answer splits three ways for an in-development hardware platform. On timing and classification, the news is good: nothing not yet sold on the EU market owes anything under the Act's 11 September reporting deadline, and a new EU implementing regulation confirms that a kitchen-adjacent connected device doesn't fall into the stricter consumer-safety product category, leaving open the cheapest self-assessment compliance route with no third-party testing fee. On support commitments, the news is less good: the European Commission's own guidance explicitly rejects five years as a default support period for hardware meant to last, pointing instead toward roughly a decade — a bar that collides with the underlying firmware toolchain's own multi-year support cycle and implies at least one forced platform migration somewhere in a device's working life. A smaller manufacturer also inherits real, if proportionate, obligations: a vulnerability-disclosure policy, a non-automated way for someone to report a flaw, a software bill of materials, and a secure update mechanism — the last of which existing open-source tooling from the chip vendor already covers. One new date worth remembering: 11 June 2028, after which a device can no longer rely on its wireless-equipment certificate alone to cover cybersecurity and needs the fuller Act-based route instead.
