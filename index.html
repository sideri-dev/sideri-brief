Today

GoPro's long strategic review has resolved into a signed, SEC-filed merger with Starman Optical — today's filing adds financing mechanics that weren't public before.

The deal was announced September 1; this is its fourth pass through our tracking, the first two instances killed as duplicates of the same announcement. New today, from GoPro's SEC proxy filed October 19: Starman pays $1.14 in cash plus 0.1 of a new share per GoPro share, backed by a $395 million financing commitment from Midtown Equities, with a $10 million termination fee if the deal collapses. It's the real-numbers resolution of a case already used as a cautionary tale against subscription-gated hardware economics — a company under prolonged strategic review ending in acquisition rather than survival on premium pricing.

Plaud, the ambient-capture note-taking wearable maker, tells the Wall Street Journal it crossed a $1 billion valuation in 2025 and is targeting a 2028 US IPO.

CEO Nathan Xu says the company will go public once annual revenue clears $1 billion; it's at $100 million in annual recurring revenue today, with more than 2.5 million users across 170-plus countries. Both outlets covering this relay the same WSJ interview rather than reporting independently, so treat the figures as single-sourced — but they're the sharpest evidence yet of how large the subscription-subsidized hardware model has grown in this category.

Acer's new Vero 16 is a mainstream Copilot+ laptop with a 50%-recycled-aluminum chassis and a battery and SSD you can swap yourself, no tools required.

Acer's own pages confirm the recycled-aluminum split, a tool-free latch for battery/SSD access, and a serviceable hinge, and state — as their own stated plan, not an independently verified teardown result — that the chassis is built to support processor upgrades across at least two hardware generations. A premium-branded, mainstream example of recycled materials and owner-serviceability shipping together without giving up premium positioning.

Yesterday's deep dive

A closer look at yesterday's memory-pricing forecast found all three of its framing claims didn't hold up: the underlying industry report turned out to be free and fully readable rather than paywalled, the "two independent outlets" covering it were relaying the same single primary report rather than reporting independently, and a claim about which storage component is affected cited a document that doesn't mention that component at all. The headline price-increase figure is a blended number driven almost entirely by enterprise SSD, while the categories a small hardware build would actually buy from are the ones the report itself calls restrained — and retail memory cards aren't covered by the report at all. A first-hand check of the Japanese retail channel found no price movement in the relevant component categories since an August price cut, but availability has shifted sharply: several Raspberry Pi storage and camera SKUs that were in stock a short time ago now read as unavailable at multiple Japanese retailers.

This week

The ambient-capture wearable category (makers like Plaud, Bee and Natura) became worth tracking as a named group this week — but a close read of their own vendor pages shows "cheap hardware plus a subscription" genuinely describes only one of the three. The real pattern across the group is subscription-gated retention of the user's own past, and records that don't survive their vendor at all: one rival's 14-day data deletion policy has no stated legal basis, another wearable destroys the raw photo it captures and keeps only an AI-generated reinterpretation, and three of the four companies that shipped a wearable like this before 2025 were gone within 22 months.

Five separate pieces of open-source and vendor software infrastructure relevant to a small AI-hardware build — a vision-model license, an on-device search component, an acquired data-sync engine, and a device-update tool — each turned out to carry their real legal or technical risk one layer away from where it would actually land, and all five moved unfavorably this week: an unlicensed re-export, a component that can't be audited in the form it actually ships, a first-party SDK that lists a Raspberry Pi board by name but forbids embedding it in anything sold commercially, a company acquisition, and a free tool that was quietly repriced to a per-device monthly fee.

For the third week running, every piece of "counter-evidence" suggesting permanent retail showrooms might make sense for a project like this fell apart on closer inspection — one well-known hardware brand's "permanent store" turned out to be a dated residency inside a venue owned by one of its own investors, under a temporary signage permit that expires next spring, and a major design trade fair was found to take no sales commission at all and actively push exhibitors toward their own websites. The one genuine finding: under Japanese law, a sale placed on a maker's own website at a pop-up event, at the maker's prompting, legally counts as a door-to-door sale rather than mail order — triggering disclosure and cooling-off-period obligations that selling purely online would otherwise avoid.
