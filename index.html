A cross-platform study finds that quantized AI models don't just run slightly differently on different chips — they can fail completely, with no error.

A new arXiv measurement study ran the same ONNX model and quantization settings across seven hardware classes — ARM and x86 CPUs, a discrete GPU, an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin's integrated GPU and NVDLA cores, and two vendor NPUs. Full-precision (FP32) predictions matched bit-for-bit everywhere. Reduced-precision INT8 predictions only matched exactly when two chips shared the same integer math kernel, and disagreed on 3.5–4.2% of predictions otherwise — and on one NPU, a bring-your-own quantized model compiled, ran and produced results with no error while its accuracy silently collapsed from 0.75 to 0.005. We're building an on-device cooking-event classifier on the ESP32-S31 using INT8 quantization; this is a strong argument to validate the model's real accuracy on that exact chip rather than trust numbers measured on a devkit or desktop.

A VOC sensor with no battery cold-starts to full power in under nine minutes, running on nothing but vibration.

Published in Nature's Microsystems & Nanoengineering, the device pairs a capacitive volatile-organic-compound sensor with a Nordic nRF52832 Bluetooth chip, powered entirely by a triboelectric nanogenerator. It cold-starts from 0V to 4.2V in 525 seconds and then runs on roughly 110 microwatts harvested from 5Hz mechanical excitation, using an energy-extraction technique the authors say harvests five times more power than a conventional rectifier. It's a live benchmark on the same air-quality-sensing territory as chips like Bosch's BME688 — useful power-budget context, even for designs (like ours) that use a conventional replaceable battery instead of energy harvesting.

A steel container reinterprets basket-weaving using parts that never actually touch.

Designer Erina Caldeira's KŌSA, covered by Core77, is built from two seamlessly-joined pieces of thin sheet steel whose crossing, weave-like elements don't physically contact each other at all — the structure holds itself together purely through the geometry of the material, with zero fasteners. It pre-orders this month at a pop-up at Matsuya Ginza in Tokyo. It's exactly the kind of structural honesty — a craft reference translated into one load-bearing material with no visible mechanism — we want our own instrument's construction to communicate.

Yesterday's deep-dive

A closer look at a premium outdoor brand's newest flagship store — announced as built around an expanded repair-and-resale centre — found the store is actually the relocation of an existing shop into the third instance of a two-year-old format, announced 17 days after it opened and never placed on the brand's own newsroom. The same brand publishes detailed investor metrics for its retail floor but zero revenue, cost or KPI figures for its repair-and-resale program in any audited filing — the only numbers live in a voluntary sustainability report, where resale growth there had halved from +48% to +24%. The brand itself co-signed an industry sustainability-foundation statement admitting that, under current economics, producing new clothes is still more profitable than repairing or reselling existing ones. In Japan, the brand's own stores refuse mail-in repair outright, restrict sewing repair to a single location, and state in writing that safety outranks longevity — a damaged garment can be refused repair entirely. The original story's framing, that this was a bet on service infrastructure over a sales floor, doesn't hold up against the evidence, and one of its key cited figures doesn't appear anywhere in the original press release.
