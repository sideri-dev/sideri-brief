Today

Japan's cultural agency just put a budget figure, in writing, behind archiving the process rather than the finished work.

The Agency for Cultural Affairs' FY2027 budget request earmarks ¥1.042 billion for a national network collecting and preserving materials generated while manga, anime, tokusatsu and games are made — drawings, storyboards, the stuff a creator throws away — not just the finished release, toward a planned "national media arts center." Its own primary budget document calls those in-between materials "an important treasure of our country... at risk of being scattered or deteriorating." The most direct, dated evidence yet of a government funding exactly this kind of in-between-state archiving.

The Getty Foundation just handed out its third round of archive-digitisation grants, naming the going rate.

Eight institutions split $1.8 million to process, digitise and open up their archives, bringing Getty's total under this initiative to $4.5 million across 20 awards since 2022 — from a historic Black-owned newspaper archive with 3 million photographs to a university home-movie collection. The per-institution average, roughly $225,000, is a real, recurring number for who actually pays to keep this kind of material usable.

An open-source toolkit claims the best memory-for-speed trade-off yet for searching huge collections of images by similarity.

Two new Rust libraries — Vectorium for storing and compressing each image's numerical fingerprint, kANNolo for searching them fast — report the best memory/throughput balance on 9 of 12 tested combinations, matching or beating established tools like Faiss. A direct hit on the resource constraint any on-device, no-label image search has to live inside — though the code's own licence field on GitHub is blank, so it isn't usable commercially until that's resolved.

Researchers spent seven nights laser-scanning and photographing a cathedral in Malta, end to end.

99,000 images from DSLRs, a drone and LIDAR, shot at night to avoid crowds, built into a 25–30 billion-triangle reconstruction that had to cope with reflective metal, dark materials and restricted access — a real-world ceiling for what capturing an entire heritage building costs in time and volume, useful scale against any lighter-weight capture approach.

SoftBank just showed how cheaply a handheld scanner and a drone can assess earthquake damage from a distance.

Working with a small town in Wakayama under a national smart-city program, SoftBank built 3D digital twins of damaged buildings from a handheld 3D scanner and an autonomous drone, then had engineers judge structural risk remotely — catching cracks as fine as 2mm and tilts of about one degree. Government-funded, run three times, and a genuine cost/hardware comparison point for anything built on cheaper, camera-based capture.

Yesterday, revisited

An earlier deep-dive re-tested a widely-shared preprint claiming a frozen vision transformer can separate objects in a photo with no labels at all, and the claim turns out narrower than first reported. The method still needs ground-truth labels to pick which attention head and clustering setting to use — only the moment-to-moment grouping itself is label-free. Scored on the full standard benchmark rather than a filtered subset, the headline score drops from .615 to .410, level with a 2023 model and behind a 2024 one on every other metric. It's an unreviewed single-lab submission with no published code, and nothing shipped anywhere — including the one real museum deployment found in production — yet separates objects within a photo without either a labelled-box detector or a scale too small to reach a full collection.
