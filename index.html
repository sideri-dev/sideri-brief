Today

Singapore's planning authority gazetted a 1920s bungalow for conservation while clearing most of the surrounding site for private housing — and nobody is recording what else was there.

Singapore's Urban Redevelopment Authority formally approved conservation status on October 21 for the Haji Kahar bungalow, built in the 1920s by the man locals called the "King of Bedok," on a roughly 5,900–6,000 sq m freehold site now slated for "premium private landed housing." The same site holds a separate, undesignated kampong-style house, and neither the authority's own filing nor any press coverage found mentions any plan to document it before the rest of the site clears. It's the sharpest live example yet of a recurring question in heritage work — who decides what gets a record before redevelopment, and what happens to everything that falls outside the gate.

The UK's National Lottery Heritage Fund is paying £45,698 over three years for a digital asset management system.

The contract, awarded to a firm called Brandstencil, runs September 2026 through August 2029, and was only recoverable from the government's machine-readable procurement data after the ordinary notice page blocked a plain fetch. It's a small but genuine data point on what a national heritage-funding body actually budgets for archive software — much smaller in scale than the UK's separate, much larger Preservica contract with the British Library reported two days ago.

A century of Italian design history — tubular-steel furniture, bicycles, patents, prototypes — just got its own private archive in Milan, open by appointment only.

The new Colombo Archive holds tens of thousands of documents, drawings, catalogues, photographs and prototypes from the manufacturer's history, and its own stated intent is explicit: not a museum, not a visitor centre, nothing aimed at the general public. It's a clean precedent for what an owned process archive can look like — a maker keeping its own design history as a managed, selectively-opened asset rather than donating it outward or putting it on permanent public display.

A new paper argues the thing that actually breaks cross-scale image search isn't resolution — it's the mismatch between how big an object is and how big the picture around it is.

The fix — query-side scale augmentation plus an OWLv2 cropping re-ranker — reaches state-of-the-art results on a 100-million-image benchmark (ILIAS) without retraining the underlying model or rebuilding the index. For any system trying to match similar images with no labels at all, that's exactly the kind of cheap fix worth tracking: it improves accuracy on top of what already exists rather than demanding a new model or a rebuilt database.

A second new model, ELViS, re-ranks similarity results so visual search keeps working accurately even on image types its encoder never saw in training.

Published at ICLR 2026, it's built to plug into several already-popular open image encoders (DINOv2, DINOv3, SigLIP2) and ships with ready-to-use checkpoints, no retraining required. For a search system meant to handle whatever someone actually photographs — not a fixed, pre-defined category — that ability to generalize into unseen territory without retraining is directly useful groundwork.

Yesterday's deep-dive

Yesterday's deep-dive tested a Japanese government council's draft report, which the previous day's brief had read as a policy review of Japan's building-designation regime — and found that reading wrong. The document is actually the wrap-up of a five-year craft-skills preservation plan and a design brief for its successor; words like photograph, drawing, redevelopment, demolition and loss appear zero times across its 31 pages. It isn't final either — a further council session follows on November 25, with the actual report due in December. Between the draft and the final text, language about undesignated properties was trimmed back at one member's request, and a sentence treating accumulated image and 3D data as an asset was cut outright. The budget confirms the shape: seven identifiable documentation lines total only about ¥495 million against a ¥233.5 billion funding request, most of which was already earmarked for the next five-year plan before the council had even finished designing it.
