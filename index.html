Today

Japan's Cultural Affairs Agency holds the final session of its advisory council today on a draft national policy for preserving and using cultural properties.

The council — the 文化審議会文化財分科会企画調査会, meeting for the ninth and apparently last time — votes this afternoon on a draft report on, in effect, "the future shape of preserving and using cultural properties," the policy track behind Japan's designation system that decides which buildings and materials get a mandated record before redevelopment. (A small irony: the agency's own page can't agree with itself on a single character in the draft's own title.) This bears directly on who ends up requiring, and paying for, an archival record of a building before it's torn down — exactly the question at the center of our own archiving work.

Google published Ginger, a new open-source library for nearest-neighbor search, claiming a 2.6x memory cut and 2.5x graph compression over the previous state of the art.

Google also claims query speeds 1.8x faster than each benchmark dataset's previous best competitor. That's exactly the compression problem any on-device, label-free visual-search system eventually has to solve — a similarity index that keeps growing has to still fit on modest hardware, and an index this much smaller is real evidence of how far that's movable. It's Google's own paper with no independent replication yet, but that's normal for a library released days ago.

A Denver court has resolved a legal fight over 50 terabytes of archive footage trapped with a storage vendor that vanished mid-contract — the public broadcaster gets its files back.

The judge ordered the facility holding the data to cooperate with a third-party extraction, despite having no direct contract with the broadcaster, once the broadcaster pays the vanished vendor's overdue storage fees. More than 11,000 files spanning seven decades were at stake. It's a live case study in exactly the failure mode a maker-held, one-time-hardware archive is built against: when a third party holds your creative record and disappears, getting it back takes a lawsuit and a judge, not a login.

Shimane Prefecture opened an exhibition explicitly billed as building an architectural-materials archive, centered on Metabolist architect Kiyonori Kikutake's donated design materials.

Kikutake's family donated his architectural design materials to the prefecture in 2025, and the prefecture states its goal in plain terms: organizing them into a public architectural-materials archive. It's a clean, named example of exactly the in-between, pre-public creative material we track, and a possible institutional precedent for how this kind of material gets preserved and shown.

Digital-asset-management vendor Third Light has fully shut down, and its last customers are reportedly being charged $2,500 just to get their own files off the platform on a USB drive.

Remaining customers are being force-migrated to the acquirer's own platform, PhotoShelter for Brands, with replacement contracts reportedly starting around $10,000 a year. The dollar figures come from a competing vendor's own blog post, so treat those two numbers as one-sided; the shutdown and forced migration themselves are independently confirmed elsewhere. It's a concrete price tag on what it costs when you don't hold your own archive.

Yesterday's deep-dive

Yesterday's deep-dive tested Pinterest's new ad unit inside visual search against Pinterest's own documents. It turns out to be bought with keywords against a product catalogue, not bid on an image or an embedding — so the "taste and intent, auction-biddable" framing has no advertiser-side mechanism behind it, and whether the paid placement actually reorders the organic results is an unusually complete documented silence across thousands of help-center pages. Pinterest's own 2020 announcement already shows ads running inside visual search results, directly contradicting its CEO's October 2026 claim that this was new. Revenue per user in the region spanning Japan and Southeast Asia runs at roughly an eighth of the global rate, and no other major platform — Google, Microsoft, Snap, TikTok, Amazon — runs ads inside an image-similarity surface at all. Right now the only priced markets for image similarity anywhere are wholesale cloud infrastructure and tendered institutional archiving contracts, not consumer ad auctions.
