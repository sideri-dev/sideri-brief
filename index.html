Today

An industrial research group, AIRoA, working jointly with Toyota Motor's own Future Creation Center, built a handheld rig that straps a fisheye camera onto each of a worker's hands just to record how a task is actually performed.

The rig, called YUBI, tracks hand posture with VR equipment, records tasks that involve using tools, and runs across dozens of these rigs in parallel to build training data for robot foundation models. Independently confirmed by the group's own press release and a separate academic paper describing the same system. It's a clean, concrete example of passively capturing a hands-on work process into structured, replayable data — built here for an employer's own training pipeline rather than for an individual user.

A new research benchmark pins down the precise video frame where a hand touches an object, beating prior methods by nearly 17 points of precision.

The "TouchMoment" dataset and detector covers more than 8,400 annotated hand-contact moments across over 4,000 egocentric videos, evaluated under a strict two-frame tolerance, and posts a 16.91% average-precision gain over existing event-spotting approaches. It's a purpose-built answer to a problem that matters for any camera device trying to decide the exact instant something worth recording has happened — most existing work only approximates it with generic motion or occlusion signals.

A new egocentric-video retrieval system goes further than "find the clip" — it tries to infer what the viewer actually meant and ranks results against a personalized memory of what they've seen before.

Introduced at the ACL 2026 conference with a 639-query benchmark built from real users' own object annotations, the system reportedly beats prior approaches by leaning on exactly that kind of interpretive inference. It's a useful data point for anyone weighing a "search tool that only answers what's asked" design against one that actively guesses and ranks on the user's behalf — opposite design philosophies for the same underlying problem.

Granola, an AI note-taking tool, just raised $125 million at a $1.5 billion valuation — roughly six times what it was worth ten months earlier.

Index Ventures led the round, and the company's revenue is reported to have grown 2.5x since January; both the valuation jump and the funding figures are independently confirmed by a funding report and the lead law firm's own announcement of the deal. It's a sharp, current valuation benchmark for note-taking and personal-archive-style software tools more broadly.

Framework's promise not to pass rising component costs onto customers is running into its own stockroom.

The laptop maker extended a memory price cut to everyone still waiting on a Laptop 13 Pro order, but admits it has run out of the cheaper-memory stock it used to absorb that cost — so pre-built Laptop 13 and 16 configurations are "likely to get more expensive in the coming months," with one configuration already back up to $1,899. It's a live test of how far a "we won't be extractive" pledge survives contact with a genuine supply shock, relevant to anyone building hardware under the same memory and storage cost pressure right now.

Yesterday's deep-dive

An earlier deep-dive tested a newly released on-device retrieval feature from Google's MediaPipe library on a Raspberry Pi 5, and found it real but narrower than it first looked. The feature genuinely ships, tagged October 6th, and a first measured figure on a Pi 5 — 1,761 milliseconds and 619MB of memory per call — is fast enough to matter but rules out running it on a much smaller fallback board. The claim that this single release now covers two separate jobs in one dependency didn't hold up, though: it still quietly pulls in a separate computer-vision library regardless, so there's no real consolidation, and the "vector database" underneath turns out to be a brute-force scan with no faster search path exposed. One correction cut the other way — the underlying embedding model's license is more permissive than first reported, removing a real terms-of-use concern.
