Today

Core Devices' Pebble Round 2 sold out its second watch and immediately hit a hardware defect — the recovery numbers are now public.

A cosmetic CNC-milling indentation on the stainless case forced Core Devices to halt mass production this spring. Roughly 14,000 pre-orders are now working through a roughly two-month rebuild, most shipping by the end of September and the rest in early October, run by a three-person support team across 93 countries. It's still sold direct-to-consumer, with no crowdfunding platform anywhere in the picture. It's a real, dated measure of how long recovery actually takes after a precision-machined metal part fails late in tooling — worth a closer look at how small hardware makers survive that kind of hit.

Vermicular raised prices 3–5% on its core cast-iron cookware line, and said plainly why.

Its own announcement blames rising materials and logistics costs, not demand, across the Oven Pot 1, Oven Pot 2 and Soup Pot lines, effective from August 3. It's one of the clearest examples going of a premium Japanese kitchenware maker communicating a cost-driven price increase honestly rather than dressing it up in marketing language.

A new compression technique put a real neural network on a microcontroller with 2 kilobytes of memory.

"Deep Microcompression" combines structured pruning, quantization-aware training and bit-packing to shrink a LeNet-5 image-recognition network 55.8-fold while holding 98.77% accuracy, and researchers say it's the first documented deployment of a standard convolutional neural network on the ATmega328P — a chip long considered far too small for that job. It's a compression technique demonstrated on small hobbyist-grade silicon, not a benchmark on more capable chips, but it's a sign that classifier workloads keep getting cheaper to run on tiny hardware faster than expected.

A ¥19,800 paper toy is a clean case study in how much a purely tactile object can charge on feel alone.

PÉPET, designed by Masae Nagao and produced by Japanese paper company Takeo Paper, is built from over 400 layered circles of a fine paper called NT Rasha, offered in 120 colorways, and ruffles and moves when stroked — no electronics anywhere. It sells for roughly 20 times the price of the raw paper craft it's built from, purely on interaction quality.

Yesterday's deep-dive

A closer look at Tokyo's AI-sorting battery recycling box found the box itself was the least interesting part of the story. What actually exists are three separate, time-boxed pilot programs, not one standing scheme, each run with a different waste-handling partner, and none of them cites any specific law on its own page. On the underlying legal-obligation question, the finding is a clean no: Japan's small-appliance recycling law places essentially no enforceable duty on an importer-seller, and its scope language — limited to items "for the general consumer's ordinary use" — may not even reach a creator's or professional instrument. The one thing on this ground genuinely worth tracking is still proposed rather than enacted: Japan's environment ministry is committed to amending its waste-management rules by the end of the current fiscal year to require disclosure of lithium-battery content in industrial-waste outsourcing contracts, which would reach any business handling electronics scrap in the country.
