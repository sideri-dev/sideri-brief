Yesterday's deep-dive

A closer read of Japan's radio-certification display rules found the underlying legal picture is better than the corpus had assumed: showing a certification mark digitally on a device screen, with no physical mark and no packaging, has been a fully legal, unconditioned display method since 2010 — not a workaround awaiting approval. What's genuinely live is timing: a July 2026 cabinet decision sets a March 2027 deadline for the government to conclude a broader review of display-method rules, a year ahead of a related reform on the same table, and no rule text has been drafted yet. The read also surfaces a gap the on-screen approach doesn't close on its own: secondhand buyback counters verify a certification mark by physically inspecting the device, and a screen behind a dead battery reads as unverifiable — a real resale-value problem for a product meant to be kept and passed on.

This week

Every readiness gate on the ESP32-S31 — the chip just chosen for production — moved this week, and all of them moved against the ability to build.

The pinned firmware toolchain can't compile for the part at all yet; the version upgrade that would fix that missed its plan date (see above); the chip sits outside every official vendor support window; and an unpatched silicon-level security defect with no available fix now applies to it. Distributor stock exists, but not until late January 2027. None of this reverses the chip choice, but it turns the pending firmware version bump from a routine update into the actual gate on writing any firmware for the part.

Three regulatory tracks — Japanese certification, the EU's Cyber Resilience Act, and Japan's IPA-run JC-STAR security-labeling scheme — are all converging on the same small on-device settings screen.

All three want content on that surface: a support end-date, update controls, a data-erase path, a certification number. The sensible move is to design it once, as a single identity-and-lifecycle panel — but a secondhand buyback counter verifies by physically inspecting the device, which a settings-screen-only approach doesn't solve on its own.

What a maker owes customers after the sale is turning from a policy question into a number that has to appear on the price page.

Japanese industry practice and EU law converged independently this week on roughly a decade as the right order of magnitude for how long hardware support should run. EU law makes the figure legally supervised and requires it be shown at the point of purchase; the comparables funding support tails through subscriptions or abrupt cutoffs are both visibly struggling. An architecture that keeps everything local, with nothing running through the cloud, is what makes committing to a long support number affordable in the first place.
