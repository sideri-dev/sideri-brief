Espressif's own advisory puts a chip we've chosen for production hardware on an unpatched Secure Boot bypass list.

Espressif's security advisory AR2026-006 confirms the ESP32-S31 was added to the list of chips affected by a ROM-level ECDSA Secure Boot signature-validation bug back in July, with no software fix available — Espressif's own recommendation is to configure Secure Boot with RSA instead of ECDSA for new production batches, and the ECDSA path isn't even exposed in the chip's software framework yet. Exploiting it needs flash-write access (supply chain or physical tampering), so it isn't a remote-network risk, but it's a real firmware-architecture question for anyone building on this chip today.

GoPro's $285M sale to an optics and defense company closes out a long decline.

GoPro has agreed to merge into Starman Optical for $1.14 a share, leaving GoPro's own shareholders with roughly 10% of the combined company and wiping $92 million of debt — the culmination of a long slide this brief has tracked across multiple cycles. The combined company is aiming at "consumer, commercial and defense markets." It's a sharp reminder of what happens to a hardware brand once its core product becomes commoditized: near-total shareholder dilution, and a pivot into an unrelated business just to clear the books.

Sharp shows what "AI in the kitchen" costs in Japan today.

Sharp is launching "NIAH," a cloud generative-AI agent spanning 52 appliance models from September 30, replacing its existing COCORO HOME AI assistant, with a free tier plus paid tiers at ¥495, ¥990 and ¥1,980 a month. It's the market's current default answer to smart-appliance AI — cloud-based, generative, subscription-priced — and a useful benchmark for anyone weighing a local, one-time-purchase alternative against it.

A Tokyo shop sells "how it's made" as the product itself.

AXIS profiled MA STORE, a small Setagaya retailer where owner Matsuo Tsubasa runs a "running-alongside" partnership model with independent makers — repeated dialogue to find where a maker's skill meets what they genuinely want to create, paired with an explicit three-way revenue split between materials, maker and seller that Matsuo calls the key to keeping Japanese manufacturing alive. It's a concrete, real-price example of visible craft functioning as the actual sales pitch, not just packaging around it.

Yesterday's deep-dive

Balmuda's wind-down of its Phone line turns out to be two contradicting deadlines rather than one clean one: repairs got over two years of notice, but the app and cloud features got 75 days, and every feature dying on schedule — weather, currency conversion, voice input — turns out to depend on a remote server, while the calculator's own arithmetic, needing no server at all, keeps working. Japan imposes no legal duty to keep parts, updates or service alive for a product like this; the multi-year support figures people commonly cite trace back to a non-binding industry-association table that doesn't even cover phones. The UK and the EU do impose real support-disclosure requirements — the EU's incoming Cyber Resilience Act explicitly treats a product's cloud dependency as part of the product itself, with a multi-year support floor. The broader lesson: architecture decided at design time determines exactly what a device is worth on the day a company walks away from it, and a design that keeps its core functions running entirely on-device can promise something most connected hardware today cannot.
