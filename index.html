Today

Pinterest just put ad auctions inside visual search itself, run entirely on "taste and intent" — the opposite of the no-ranking approach we're building toward.

Pinterest's new Visual Search Ads put advertisers directly into search results and pin closeups, powered by what the company calls "Pinterest Intelligence — our AI-enabled visual understanding of images, products, taste and intent," across more than 80 billion searches a month, most of them visual. The business is working: Q3 2026 revenue hit $1.174 billion, up 12% year over year, on a record 661 million monthly active users. We're building a visual-search system that works the other way around — matching images purely by measured visual similarity, never by popularity, taste, or inferred intent — and Pinterest's numbers are the clearest real-world test yet of what that trade-off costs against a fully monetized, ranked alternative.

The British Library signed a £2.4 million, six-year contract with Preservica to rebuild what its 2023 cyber-attack destroyed.

UK government procurement records confirm the deal directly: £2,398,500 excluding VAT, running from early 2026 to early 2032 with a possible extension to 2037, to "re-enable essential strategic collecting capabilities that suffered deep and extensive disruption after a cyber-attack on the Library in 2023." It's a clean, publicly disclosed institutional budget for exactly the kind of digital-preservation capability we track — a subscription-software sale, worth sitting next to any one-time-hardware approach to the same problem.

Japan's Cultural Affairs Agency is paying up to ¥15 million to document and curate the archive of architect Takahashi Teiichi for a 2027 exhibition.

The agency's own national archive of modern architecture is tendering out the exhibition planning and preparation work for Takahashi Teiichi and his firm Dai-ichi Kobo, covering drawings, photographs and footage already in its custody, with the budget cap and scope confirmed directly from the tender document. It's a government archive publicly paying, at a disclosed price, to turn a creator's working papers into a public record — exactly the kind of service we're trying to understand the economics of.

A new paper shows two vision models can score identically on standard tests and still behave completely differently once you actually search with them.

The paper finds that some self-supervised encoders produce embeddings that are badly shaped for approximate-nearest-neighbor search — "degrade the performance of partition-based and hashing-based search" — even when their accuracy on usual benchmarks looks fine; what decides search performance is the geometry of the embedding space, not the headline accuracy number. That's a real design trap for any on-device, label-free similarity search system: picking an encoder by the usual leaderboard could quietly produce an index that searches badly, despite scoring well on a test set.

Japan's Cultural Affairs Agency is studying what to do with the one surviving building of the old Osaka Army Arsenal — a building that probably isn't even formally protected.

Everything else at the site was destroyed in the final day of WWII bombing or demolished afterward; this one chemical-analysis building survived, passed through government and university use, and held a Self-Defense Force office until 1994. The new tender is for a feasibility study, not a protection order, and the building appears to carry no formal heritage designation at all — a real piece of heritage sitting in a pre-decision gap where nothing requires a record to be made before its fate is decided.

Yesterday's deep-dive

A closer look this week tested a Milwaukee ordinance requiring laser scanning before a historic building's demolition, against the primary legal record — and most of the public story doesn't hold up. The enacted law never says "laser" or "scan" and defers every technical standard to commission guidelines that, seven months on, don't exist anywhere in the public record, so the permit condition it's supposed to enforce can't currently be applied as written. The widely quoted $800 price and "who pays" answer trace to one person — a sitting preservation commissioner who also operates the scanner and chairs the committee writing the missing standards — not to any city document. The US National Park Service's own documentation standards explicitly state that a scan "does not meet the Secretary of the Interior's Standards" and "is not considered formal documentation," directly contradicting the ordinance's claim that a scan counts as "a permanent digital twin or equivalent archival digital representation." It was also built to apply to fewer than one building a year, and nothing has triggered it yet.
