Meta's own internal strategy memo is reportedly planning an AI-powered pendant, built on last year's acquisition of Limitless, aimed at 10 million unit sales in the second half of 2026.

The pendant sits alongside a business-subscription wearables plan — arriving even as Meta's Reality Labs division posted a $4.62 billion operating loss on just $431 million of revenue last quarter. No standalone ambient-capture startup operates anywhere near that scale. It's the sharpest evidence yet that the nearer-term threat to a premium capture device like the one we're building isn't a scrappy startup — it's a platform absorbing the whole category as a subsidized feature of something much bigger.

A funded startup called Veritas is building a tap-to-verify authenticity chip for luxury and craft goods — $1.75 million pre-seed, led by Alexis Ohanian's venture firm Seven Seven Six.

Founder Luci Holland, a former Tesla product manager with a design and art background, built a chip that "generates a secure response with each tap" and carries a tamper-detection wire; it's already embedded in the ATHLOS championship crowns and is expanding to jewelry, trophies and glass objects. It's a genuinely different mechanism from software-based provenance standards — a physical, embedded proof bound to the object itself — and a live, funded precedent for selling authenticity as a premium feature in its own right. Worth noting: Seven Seven Six is also Teenage Engineering's third-largest shareholder.

Jaguar's first production car since its 2024 EV pivot, the Type 01, deletes the rear window entirely and replaces it with a camera system.

At 5.2 metres long on 23-inch wheels, the car is unapologetically about spectacle rather than spec sheet — CEO Rawdon Glover's own line is "people need to desire this vehicle... it's about creating irrational desire," not utility. It's a big-budget, flagship example of a camera outright replacing a traditional analog sense organ, framed in the same anti-utilitarian language a premium instrument needs to justify its price.

A new research framework called EgoCITE splits first-person video memory into two separate jobs — indexing what happened, and scoring what's relevant to a given question — and beats comparable systems by double digits at a fraction of the cost.

It cuts errors 4.4–14.2% against other agentic-memory systems while running at 36x lower cost than long-context approaches. The catch, from where we sit: its retrieval stage explicitly infers which memories matter for a given question — exactly the kind of ranking-by-intent our own retrieval design is trying to avoid, since we want a system that answers rather than selects for the user. Useful as a cost and architecture data point, not as support for that design choice.

Databricks has acquired Electric, the company behind PGlite, the most widely used local-first, in-browser Postgres database.

Weekly downloads grew from 1 million to 13 million in a year, and Databricks is folding Electric's real-time sync engine into a centralized AI-agent infrastructure product — not consumer edge devices. That's a signal the strongest independent local-first sync project at real scale no longer has a roadmap pointed at consumer hardware, which bears on whether we build a sync layer for our own local-first record in-house rather than depend on someone else's.

Yesterday's deep dive

A closer read of yesterday's item on a 1,400-page archive of a Japanese ceramics region found its headline reason for standing out — multiple independent write-ups praising its framing as "a resource for creation" — doesn't survive: that language is the publisher's own product-page copy, reproduced near-verbatim by the outlet that reported it, so it's one source, not several. The physical facts (price, release date, specs) remain genuinely independent. More to the point for our own thinking: the book has no index, table of contents, digital counterpart or cross-reference scheme, explicitly disclaims being a dictionary or encyclopedia, and its own publisher describes its actual uses as sales talk, staff training and display material — not reference lookup. It ships only the finished, composed artefact, with no readable inputs behind it, the same failure mode as a maker who kept the negatives but lost the photographs.
