Japan just extended biometric-privacy protection to cover exactly what a passive capture device does.

A cabinet-approved amendment to Japan's Personal Information Protection Act, passed by the lower house on May 26, lets anyone request that use of their personal information stop if it contains facial-feature data — even if the handling was otherwise lawful. It bears directly on any device built to passively capture a maker's process on camera: if someone else happens to be in frame, their facial data is now covered regardless of how clean the handling otherwise is. Worth a closer look as a case study of where privacy law is heading for always-on capture hardware.

Raspberry Pi's own blog just proved a real function-calling model runs on a Pi 5 with no accelerator.

Needle 2, a 14MB model built on cactus-needle 2.0.7, runs on a Raspberry Pi 5, CPU only, at 488/296 tokens per second prefill/decode — no GPU, no NPU, no cloud round-trip. We're building on the Raspberry Pi 5, so this is direct evidence that a local-first, on-device design can handle real actions without phoning home. (The post's own page carries two conflicting publish dates; treat September 22 as the reliable one.)

CamScanner shipped its first hardware product, and it's a close comparable.

CamScanner — the document-scanning software company, self-described as the world's largest AI product for image-based text processing with 300M+ users — launched the Apex 1 Pro scanner on Kickstarter at $359 early-bird. It flattens pages, removes stray fingers and reduces glare entirely on-device, during capture. Founder Anton Tan's own framing: "People don't want another camera—they want digital documents that are ready to use." A close read on what a capture-first hardware thesis looks like from a software-native company, launching through crowdfunding rather than a direct storefront.

Oura's IPO filing is the clearest public data yet on what a hardware-plus-membership business actually retains.

The S-1 filed with the SEC discloses hardware revenue of $973.98M against membership revenue of $240.5M for the nine months to June 30, and a 12-month paid-member retention rate of approximately 85%. The reported $16B+ valuation is press-sourced, not stated in the filing itself, which is normal ahead of pricing. An 85% retention figure on an attached software layer is a useful real-world number for any hardware maker weighing a recurring revenue tier.

Looki L1 is the sharpest possible mirror for a retrieval-only design philosophy — and it's the exact opposite of it.

This $199–249 wearable camera "picks the highlights" of your day and "edits them automatically into a vlog" under what Looki calls "Proactive Intelligence": auto-select, auto-edit, auto-narrate. A retrieval-first design does none of that by philosophy — it answers, it never selects, ranks, or suggests. Worth tracking as the clearest counter-example in the market to that idea. One loose end: Looki's own site prices the device at $249 today, while CES coverage from January quoted $199 — unresolved, most likely a price increase since launch, but neither source says so directly.

Yesterday's deep-dive

A deep-dive into a supplier-shortage story found it had been overstated the first time around: a strainer maker's craftsmanship story, framed as a lost technique reconstructed from scratch, turned out to be an eight-day-old repeat whose own dedupe check falsely claimed it was new, and whose central claims didn't hold up against the very page cited for them — the workshop's loom was rebuilt on-site by a craftsman, not relocated, and the two outlets claimed as corroboration actually contradict the account. The underlying shortage phenomenon is real and officially measured, but the mechanism turns out to be demographic rather than commercial — most closures hit profitable firms with aging owners, which breaks any screening strategy keyed to supplier financial health. The same failure pattern is already live one tier under real hardware: a widely used camera module's own EEPROM chip went end-of-life in a January 2026 change notice, its function silently dropped from the module, with customers told to re-engineer around it. Across five historical reconstruction cases studied, none was ever driven by a record of someone actually performing the process.
