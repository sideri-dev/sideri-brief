Today

DJI just opened Japan's first DJI｜Hasselblad flagship, in Tokyo's Omotesando district today — and the two outlets covering it can't agree on who runs it.

The store (one floor of DJI hardware, one floor of Hasselblad gear, doubling as a showroom) opened this morning at La Porte Aoyama; DJI cites the street's foot traffic and an atmosphere that "matches a high-end brand like Hasselblad." But one outlet's headline calls this Japan's first DJI-directly-operated store, while a second says the opposite — "Authorized" in the name means an appointed dealer runs it, not DJI itself. Neither retracts the other. Which framing is right changes the signal: a capture-hardware brand investing directly in flagship retail, or a dealer investing in one for the brand pairing.

GoPro's own SEC filing just explained exactly how its books broke.

The restatement — already known to span four straight quarters of ineffective internal controls — traces to a warrant-valuation error: Black-Scholes models used too short an option term and a wrong illiquidity discount, overstating long-term debt by $8.1 million, plus a second miss on a convertible-debenture derivative. An independent trade outlet confirms the restated figure: $72.7 million as originally reported, $81.6 million restated.

HAY's co-founder is on record saying the point of a new lamp is that you can see how it was made.

The "Posto" table lamp, designed by Claire Lavabre, is three raw aluminium parts held together by two screws and a bulb fitting, its shot-blasted surface deliberately left showing the casting process. Co-founder Rolf Hay's own words: "I really like that you feel the material, you feel the industrial production of the cast aluminium. It's got a lot of grit to it." A named premium brand's own co-founder stating plainly that visible evidence of manufacture is the sell.

Yesterday's deep-dive

A deep-dive into a widely-read Japanese tech column on Nothing and the memory-chip shortage found it holding less new ground than it looked, and found its own central claim — how bad the shortage really is — understated, not overstated. Major memory makers' own public filings push the supply recovery later than the column assumed. One hardware maker's own books show its memory cost rising from $3.60 to $13.30 per gigabyte in six months — a sharp move, though smaller buyers' actual markup over the broader market price index turns out to be a modest 11–14%, far below the roughly 2x the original piece implied. The "exodus" framing of the column itself rests on zero actually-announced market exits.
