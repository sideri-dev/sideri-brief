A ¥29,800 Japanese AI earphone just showed what a Makuake-to-retail launch in this price class actually earns.

SB C&S's GLIDiC AI +u Buds — funded past ¥15M by more than 600 Makuake backers — moved to general retail today at ¥29,800 (¥42,048 with a year of the Pro plan), heading into SoftBank shops from September 4. It records conversations three ways, transcribes locally on-device, and leans on an explicit trust pitch: storage on domestic servers that is never used to train AI. It's a close comparable for anyone building a device that captures a moment and hands back an AI interpretation of it — pricing, feature set, and privacy framing all in one place, which is exactly why it's what we're digging into next.

Samsung wants a 20% DRAM price hike for Q3 — manufacturers say they're out of room to pass it on.

Samsung has verbally notified clients of a roughly 20% contract-price increase; TrendForce's independent forecast lands lower, 13–18% for DRAM generally, with LPDDR5X-class parts most likely to hit the full 20%. This is the third straight quarter of aggressive memory increases, after more than 90% in Q1 and 50–60% in Q2. For anyone sourcing memory-bearing components right now, Q3–Q4 2026 quotes should be treated as a moving negotiation, not a settled baseline.

Fairphone brought its modular, repairable phone to the US for the first time, at $649 — pitched explicitly against today's memory crunch.

The Gen 6+ launch runs a Snapdragon 7s Gen 4 with 12GB of RAM, has 12 owner-replaceable parts (battery, cameras, USB-C port, display) swappable with a single screwdriver, and carries a five-year warranty. TechCrunch frames the whole pitch around the same component-shortage environment as the DRAM story above. It's a live pricing and positioning precedent for any premium device landing in that same environment — repairability as a longevity argument, and a concrete benchmark for how far component-level serviceability can go.

The 256GB model drops from ¥119,900 to ¥95,990 and the 512GB from ¥138,900 to ¥108,900, with MNP and installment campaigns pushing effective pricing as low as ¥55,990 — through Nothing's sole Japanese carrier, same-day. Espressif open-sourced its entire device-to-cloud-to-app stack, ESP RainMaker Neo, under Apache 2.0.

The rebuilt stack — cloud backend, firmware SDK, mobile app SDK, dashboard — deploys into a developer's own AWS account instead of Espressif's hosted infrastructure, across three tiers: a public hosted "Neo," an own-AWS "packaged" option, and full build-from-source. It's a plausible backbone for anyone building on the ESP32-S3 who wants to avoid vendor lock-in and keep control of their own data. It's also a week-old release with no migration path from the older, still-supported Classic stack it replaces — worth tracking, not committing to yet.

Yesterday's deep-dive — Balmuda

Balmuda's decline was a sequence, not one mistake: a founder-conviction phone launch in 2021 at flagship price with mid-range hardware, a formal exit 18 months later that founder Gen Terao attributed to "our own insufficient capability," and a five-year revenue slide (¥18.4bn to ¥10.1bn, down 45%) tracking a shrinking Japanese premium-appliance market. The core lesson: a 3–5x category price premium survives where the buyer's comparison is experiential — the toaster — and dies where it's a rigid spec-per-yen comparison, as the phone found out against the smartphone giants. Balmuda's 2025–26 pivot toward limited-run, high-price design objects sold mostly overseas (a ¥550,000 lantern made with Jony Ive's LoveFrom studio, a ¥59,400 bedside clock) is now the only growing line in the business — a real-time case study for anyone building a small-batch, export-led premium hardware product of their own.
