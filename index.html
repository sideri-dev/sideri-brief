Today

Espressif's ESP32-S31 chip now carries a Secure Boot defect on its official, permanent errata page rather than only in a one-off security advisory.

The issue itself was already disclosed in July: ECDSA-signed Secure Boot on the chip incorrectly accepts signatures with invalid values, and the only workaround until a future silicon revision is to use RSA-based Secure Boot instead. What's new is where it now lives — Espressif's standing per-chip errata documentation, the kind of page that stays live and gets checked by anyone building on the part, rather than a bulletin that ages out of view.

Shopify is ending "pay duty at the door" as an option across its Managed Markets platform — checkout duty collection is now the default everywhere it's supported.

As of August 24, any store relying on delivered-duty-unpaid shipping gets automatically converted to delivered-duty-paid: customers see and pay import tax and duty at checkout rather than being billed by a courier on delivery. It's a meaningful shift for any direct-to-consumer hardware brand shipping internationally — the platform itself is increasingly handling landed-cost calculation natively rather than leaving it to the merchant or the customer to sort out at the border.

Yesterday's deep-dive

An investigation into the EU's Cyber Resilience Act found that, two days before its vulnerability-reporting rule takes effect, the actual operating rulebook is an ENISA web page rather than the regulation's own text — no formal legal instrument yet exists to govern it. The real cost of compliance turns out to be procedural rather than technical: exactly one named individual per company needs a personal EU Login account with two-factor authentication to file reports, and no backup contact can be added until that person is separately verified by national cybersecurity authorities — a single point of failure by design. The launch version of the reporting platform is also thinner than advertised, with web-form-only submission, no proper API, and at least one required field the platform doesn't yet contain, which is the documented cause of a known bug in the regulator's own 72-hour reporting deadline. Separately, the European Commission's own guidance documents disagree with each other about whether a finished-device maker or the maker of an underlying chip component bears the reporting duty when a vulnerability is found in shared firmware infrastructure — meaning liability for a flaw in third-party crypto code would likely fall on whoever ships the finished product, not the chip vendor.
