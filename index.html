1. A Japanese national cultural-heritage platform already connects collection images by pure visual similarity, no labels, in production since 2024.

Japan Search's "lab" feature places thumbnails from almost every item in the national registry near others that look visually similar, built on a modified version of the open-source "deepscatter" visualization engine, live since March 2024. One nuance: the text labels shown on each cluster are auto-extracted from item titles, even though the placement itself runs purely on visual similarity. It's a rare example of this kind of image-clustering shipping at national-archive scale rather than staying a research prototype.

2. A Japanese prefecture's architectural-archive exhibition turns out to rest on two separate finds, not one — including blueprints discovered by accident in its own basement.

Shimane Prefecture's exhibition pairs the 2025 donation of architect Kiyonori Kikutake's design materials by his family with a second, previously unreported find: the prefecture's own headquarters blueprints, discovered by staff in a basement storeroom back in 2023. Both sets are now headed for a planned public architecture archive. It's a reminder that process material — drawings, sketches, working papers — often survives by accident, long before anyone decides to formally preserve it.

3. The Salvador Dalí Foundation has put a concrete number on what digitising an artist's full legacy costs: about €9 per item.

The foundation committed €1.2 million over three years to digitise roughly 133,000 items — 118,000 documents plus nearly 15,000 heritage objects — generating more than 600,000 individual image captures. Named institutional digitisation budgets at this level of detail are rare, and this one is a real benchmark for what archiving a creative legacy actually costs at scale. It's reported by one outlet only; no second source carrying the same figures has turned up.

4. Rhizome is shutting down its hosted web-archiving tool Conifer — "not a sunset, it's twilight."

In June 2026, a permanent, read-only "tombstone" application replaces Conifer: existing collections stay accessible, but nobody can add to or edit them again. A follow-up post in May confirmed the shutdown stayed on schedule. It's a clean illustration of the risk built into archiving on someone else's hosted platform — when the vendor exits, growing that archive further stops being possible, whatever happens to the material already stored there.

5. The architect Paul R. Williams's archive is debuting across three Los Angeles institutions at once, not one, six years after it was acquired.

The Getty Research Institute and USC's Fisher Museum of Art each run their own separate exhibition of the Williams archive, on their own dates, alongside the previously known Los Angeles County Museum of Art show — all drawn from an archive the Getty and USC jointly acquired in 2020. A minor date discrepancy, over whether the run ends May 23 or May 31, 2027, remains unresolved, though this source sides with the earlier date. Six years between acquisition and public debut says something on its own about how long archival material can sit in institutional custody before it reaches the public.

Yesterday's deep dive

A deep-dive into the RELAY paper (arXiv:2611.31882), which claims to let a visual-search system swap in a better image encoder without re-encoding its whole existing gallery, found the claim overstated: RELAY's method still re-encodes every document once — what it avoids is re-encoding before an interim accuracy gain arrives. The technique also doesn't transfer well beyond its own setup: it works by modifying language-model layers, it needs text captions rather than pure images, and it was only tested on a gallery of 5,000 images. Its one genuinely useful finding is a warning: a partially migrated similarity index, scored on a mix of old and new vectors, can rank worse than the untouched original system — meaning any such index upgrade needs every stored vector tagged by which model produced it, with the old index kept serving until a migration fully completes rather than blended partway through.
