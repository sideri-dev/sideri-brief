A Japanese ceramics archive, 14 years and 1,400 pages in the making, is being sold explicitly as "a resource for creation" — not a record of the past.

The Ceramic Valley Council and designer Satō Taku are publishing a book on Mino ware, Japan's largest ceramics-producing region: ¥29,700, boxed A4 format, out November 3, pre-sale already live at Daikanyama Tsutaya Books. Its own framing is the story — pitched against being a dictionary, encyclopedia or photo book, aiming instead to be an archive that "doesn't just preserve past accumulation but generates the next thinking, research and practice," spanning design, industry, culture and region. MIT Technology Review's on-the-ground report from Samsung's Pyeongtaek complex confirms the AI memory shortage is a multi-year structural shift, not a blip.

Three fabs already run around the clock and a fourth is coming online; a fifth, halted during the 2024 demand slump, restarted once the AI boom sent memory demand "soaring." Samsung's own internal forecast, per the piece, has demand continuing to surge through 2028 — independently echoing the same horizon other chipmakers have pointed to. The takeaway: the cost and supply pressure on memory chips isn't a short-lived spike, it's the operating environment for at least the next two years.

Core77 revisits Braun's 1960s H7 heater as a case study in how few controls a well-designed instrument actually needs.

Dieter Rams and Reinhold Weiss's control panel is held up as deliberately reduced and immediately legible — the sharpest detail is that Braun's own US-market version had to be "dumbed down" from the original, used as the cautionary counter-example. It's a clean historical argument for treating a smaller control surface as a feature, not a compromise. One caveat: independent museum records credit the H7 to Weiss alone, with no Rams co-credit confirmed elsewhere.

The Victoria and Albert Museum has formally accessioned eight of illustrator Jac Batey's working sketchbooks (2006–2011) into its permanent prints and drawings collection.

A university research record quotes the museum's own acceptance letter: eight books, 19cm×25cm, 120 pages each, described as "a fascinating visual diary and snapshot of a decade of life in Britain in the 2000s." These are working sketchbooks used to produce zines, not finished gallery art — accessioned specifically because they document a process over time, rather than a finished result. The acceptance letter is seven months old; no fresher instance of this pattern turned up this sweep.

Yesterday's deep dive

A closer look at Teenage Engineering's widely-reported "first permanent store" finds the framing wrong. 216 Lafayette Street in Manhattan is a residency venue leased and branded by a venture firm that is itself one of Teenage Engineering's own major shareholders, and Teenage Engineering is only its first resident, on a dated term running through January 24, 2027 — the city's own sign permit for the space is explicitly marked temporary, through March 1, 2027. The company lists the address on no store page, no press release, and not even its own store locator. Read correctly, it's a time-boxed brand activation inside someone else's venue, with sales still handled online — closer to a pop-up than a flagship. A different hardware brand signed an actual permanent retail lease in Manhattan this same month, and says in writing that the point of it "is not direct sales itself."
