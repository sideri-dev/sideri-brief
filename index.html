Stokke is selling a museum-colorway edition of its 1972 Tripp Trapp chair for ¥69,300

The collaboration borrows New York's Museum of Modern Art's own color identity for a limited run of the chair, which has been in MoMA's permanent collection since 2012, alongside a matching ¥12,650 cushion. The collaboration itself is confirmed by Wallpaper*, though the yen price comes from Japanese design press alone. It's a real data point for any small hardware or furniture maker weighing a museum-store collaboration as a marketing move rather than a straight sale.

A Meta Reality Labs paper gives the first full system-level power model of an always-on wearable AI device

The paper models Meta's Aria2 device end to end, and the standout finding is that power draw doesn't concentrate in one part of the system — no single component or category dominates it. We're sending this one to a deeper read, because it bears on an active question for our own always-on capture hardware's power budget, where a single academic source needs cross-checking with independent evidence before anyone relies on it.

Astrolab's upcoming lunar rover runs local language models specifically to avoid transmitting raw sensor data

FLIP, the rover, pairs an HPE Spaceborne-4 edge computer with its own distilled models so it doesn't have to send raw sensor data back to Earth — not just to dodge the Moon's communication lag. Two independent outlets confirm both the hardware and the reasoning, with only a small, non-conflicting disagreement over the exact launch date. It's an unrelated field reaching the same architectural conclusion we're building toward for our own hardware — distilling data on-device beats shipping it raw — for different reasons: physics, not privacy.

A small studio called Retro Lab is selling a $740 spherical computer monitor built from salvaged iPad screens

"Time Planet" reskins JVC's 1970 Videosphere helmet television, using 9.6-inch display panels pulled from iPads and a mechanical, AA-battery-powered flip clock built into the base. No other outlet has covered this specific product yet, so we're treating it as a single source for now — we first came across it two days earlier but couldn't verify it well enough to run it then. It's a small but real precedent for small-batch, retro-styled hardware built partly from reused components at a premium price.

Yesterday's deep dive

Yesterday we took a harder look at a paper claiming an open-source, always-on smart-glasses platform could run continuous on-device AI for 11.5 hours on a small battery through clever power management. Reading the paper's own body rather than its summary, that claim didn't hold up: the 11.5-hour figure is a straight division at near-full duty cycle with no power-gating actually implemented, the described wake-up trigger is a stated intention with no measurement behind it, and the specific hardware we're building on has no sleep state to gate anyway — only a full boot. What did hold up was more useful than the original claim: of the energy it costs to wake the system back up, the large majority goes to the camera's own startup, not the processor, which supports the simpler approach we'd already chosen over a dedicated power-gating chip. The paper's own processor vendor, GreenWaves Technologies, has been in liquidation since January 2025, and its web domain sold at auction the day before this story ran. We also caught our own earlier slip: a related paper from the same lab had already crossed our desk months before, so this wasn't really a second independent source confirming the first.
