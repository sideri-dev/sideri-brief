1. Japan's own Finance Ministry just audited how cities handle what they dig up, and most of it disappears from view.

A budget-execution audit of 134 municipalities found that for excavated artifacts that received conservation treatment, "generally undisclosed to the public" was the single most common answer — 81 of 134 cities — ahead of museum display or academic use. Separately, 32% of designated historic sites that had archaeological digs still sit unused for public access three decades later. It's a government-measured answer to who actually controls heritage records in Japan: the state pays for the dig, then routinely locks the result away — close to the opposite failure mode of the kind of device we're building, one meant to make a record easy to browse later, not harder to see.

2. A new benchmark built on 306 near-identical bronze garden-dwarf statues is the closest test yet of telling apart objects that look almost the same.

A frozen DINOv2 encoder, with no training at all, correctly names the right statue first 93% of the time by nearest-neighbor search alone — beating every one of thirteen backbones across seven training approaches tried against it. Adding a simple location weight (plausible on a fixed device that always sits in the same room) lifts that to 98.7%; comparing the two best candidates cuts false matches on unknown statues roughly in half. It's a direct, if quirky, stress test of the frozen-encoder, no-training approach this kind of visual search depends on.

3. A Sydney museum just took in a photographer's archive built entirely around her own failures.

The Chau Chak Wing Museum received an anonymous gift of Tracey Moffatt's complete photographic, film and video output since 1985, including "time capsules" of idea sketches, test prints, correspondence and film props that the museum's director says exist specifically to show "ideas that didn't work" — the first donation of its kind to any Australian museum. It's a clean, named precedent for a living artist's self-curated record of process and failure entering a permanent museum collection as its own department, confirmed by a second outlet reporting the same facts.

4. A museum in San Francisco quietly published a working example of connecting museum collections by pure image similarity, no captions.

SFO Museum's "Mills Field Digital Studio" project has released embedding files pairing its own collection with the National Gallery of Art, MoMA, four Smithsonian museums, the Cleveland Museum of Art and thirteen Flickr Commons archives, generated with Google's SigLIP2 and Apple's MobileCLIP models — vectors derived purely from pixels. Checking the page directly, the Metropolitan Museum of Art is not actually part of this at all, and a separate claim of 1.5 million embeddings in a cloud vector store couldn't be confirmed and is dropped here. What's left is still a real, named, multi-institution precedent for linking unlabeled image collections by measured visual distance at genuine scale.

5. A Bangkok redevelopment covering 22 historic buildings is already raising the question of recording what's about to change.

A master-plan renovation along Ratchadamnoen Klang Avenue recently reopened a 1950s-60s government savings-bank building, and a commentary piece on the broader plan explicitly calls for studying and recording the many post-1932 buildings in its scope alongside their renovation, rather than after the fact. It's a live, specific example of the capture-before-change problem outside Japan, though it rests on a single Thai outlet repeating a Facebook post rather than an institutional source, so the "22 buildings" figure isn't yet independently confirmed.

Yesterday's deep dive

No one has ever published a retrieval score for matching an original drawing or manga sheet against its own printed page — not in Japanese, not in English. The copy-detection benchmarks already in use score on test sets where print-and-scan is either buried inside a mixed 229-image bucket or never counted at all, so none of them actually measures this. Taken apart, added lettering is the cheapest gap ever measured, image trimming the most expensive, and halftone or screentone effects have never been measured as a retrieval problem by anyone. Real ground truth does exist, but narrowly: one Japanese manga archive writes the exact book-and-page number into its ID for roughly 3,400 sheets, and a second puts it in filenames — while the national manga book database holds over 400,000 book records and zero images.
