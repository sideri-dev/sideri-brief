Singapore's Lingverse raises $29M for a wearable camera that "recalls," not "discards," what it sees.

iKairos ships with a physical camera shutter, a privacy indicator light and a manual mute button — confirmed by the company and matched independently by two outlets. But the privacy story going around it — that the device takes occasional still photos and discards each one after analysis — doesn't hold up against the company's own words: its release says the device "can recall the visuals and bring that fleeting moment back," which is retention language, not deletion. The real story here is the reversal, not the original claim, and it's a reminder that this category's privacy promises are worth checking against the source every time.

Telekom is selling Meta's Ray-Ban Display in Germany like a phone — €1 down, then three years of installments.

Customers can pay €899 up front, or €1 followed by 36 monthly installments of €22.10 (€796.60 total) on a mobile plan. This is the highest-volume camera-wearable on the market right now, and it's reaching everyday buyers through carrier financing rather than a straight purchase — a useful data point on how a premium device in this category actually gets into people's hands at scale.

CMF by Nothing's first speakers ship with removable panels and 3D-printable replacement parts.

Speaker S ($59, a 45mm driver, 10W output) and Speaker M ($109, a 25W woofer plus 10W tweeter, 35W combined) both carry an IP68 rating, Bluetooth 6.0 and up to 18 hours of playback — confirmed across two independent outlets. The 3D-printable-parts claim comes from one source only, but the core move is real: visible modularity and user-repairability at an accessible price point, a concrete precedent for anyone thinking about repairable, user-serviceable hardware.

Yesterday's deep dive

Yesterday we highlighted a new benchmark finding that, for on-device personal-memory assistants, the choice of retrieval backend matters far more than the size of the model reading it. A closer look mostly holds that up but adds real caveats: the figures we cited came from the paper's revised version, while the version actually peer-reviewed used a smaller model and somewhat different numbers; the fast retrieval speed depends on a GPU-resident component, and on CPU alone the same step balloons from milliseconds to over two minutes; and the whole accuracy test ran on data-center hardware, not anything resembling a small edge device. One finding sharpened rather than weakened: the system's "privacy-aware" mode essentially never gates information correctly either way, which is less a privacy/utility tradeoff than evidence that a retrieval system should answer questions, not make judgment calls on its own. The architecture that won the benchmark — no AI summarization layer standing between the raw record and the query, just structured search over it directly — is structurally the same shape favored by the approach we're already taking.
