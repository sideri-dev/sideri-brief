Today

The EU's battery-removability law turns out to be stricter than earlier reporting suggested, and now has a firm date.

Reading the actual regulation text (EU 2023/1542) rather than a looser summary of it shows Article 11 bars proprietary tools entirely for removing a portable battery, requires spare batteries to stay available at a non-discriminatory price for five years after the last unit of a product ships, and bans software lock-out against a compatible replacement battery. The article takes effect 18 February 2027. It's directly relevant to any kitchen or consumer hardware being designed with a user-replaceable battery today — this is the bar a design would need to clear if it ever reaches the EU market, even for products not targeting Europe first.

Balmuda launched a ¥61,600 "Champagne Gold" special edition of its desktop clock, The Clock.

Preorders opened 25 August, wider release follows from 4 September, sold through Balmuda's own online store, its Aoyama flagship, and partner brand shops, with the same colorway following in South Korea in early October and the US in mid-October. It's a live example of how a Japanese premium-hardware brand under real revenue pressure runs a limited-color, higher-margin variant across a mix of direct and curated retail channels — worth watching for anyone building a direct-to-consumer hardware brand, as a case study rather than a template to copy outright.

A 28.9-million-parameter language model now runs locally on an ESP32-S3.

A community TinyML project got it generating text at 9 tokens per second on a roughly $8 board with 512KB SRAM, 8MB PSRAM, and 16MB flash, from a 14.9MB 4-bit model file — though it can only produce short, TinyStories-style prose, not answer questions or follow instructions. It's a concrete demonstration of what fits inside this class of chip's memory budget, relevant to anyone evaluating on-device generative text on the same hardware family we're building on, the ESP32-S3.

A research paper visualizes cooking progress in real time on a 5 TOPS edge processor.

The model synthesizes a realistic image of how a dish will look, from a raw photo plus recipe and cooking-state input, cutting image-quality error 30–60% against baselines while running at 8.68 million parameters (45MB) and 1.2 seconds per frame. It's the closest academic precedent to a problem we're working on directly — visualizing how food changes as it cooks — but it's a much heavier deep-learning approach than the lightweight statistical pattern-matching we've chosen for our own device, useful as a contrast case for why that choice still holds. Single-source (preprint, not yet peer-reviewed).

Yesterday's deep-dives

One re-examined a widely covered story about an excavator-controller interface that MIT and Sumitomo Heavy Industries built, where press coverage claimed novices performed like experts "from the start." The underlying paper's own abstract says something more modest — 37% faster cycle times at first use, "closely approximating" the performance of *trained* novices, not experts — and the two outlets repeating the stronger claim turned out to be one source restated by the other. The mechanism behind the real result does carry lessons for any interface built to close a skill gap, but only for tasks with a single correct answer; for tasks built on judgment rather than a fixed target, the better precedent is training that teaches perception and sticks even after the aid is removed, not training that spoon-feeds a live answer.

The other retested the thermal-imaging sensor market: a newer, cheaper sibling to the array we've chosen now exists from the same manufacturer, priced roughly half as much, though it isn't a strict upgrade on every spec. And at real cooking temperatures, a much higher-resolution thermal camera from a different maker turns out to be *less* accurate than our lower-resolution choice, because it has to switch into a coarser measurement mode above 140°C — turning what looked like a resolution disadvantage into an accuracy advantage. No higher-resolution option is coming from the primary manufacturer, so this piece of the design can be considered settled.
