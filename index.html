1. The UK government has opened a marketplace that sells AI companies licensed access to museum and archive collections.

UK Research and Innovation, working with the Natural History Museum, has launched a pilot marketplace — built by Harbr Group Limited under a roughly £600,000 contract — meant to give archive owners "a secure, trusted way to connect with buyers at scale," including AI companies, on standardised licensing terms. Twelve institutions are already in, from Historic England and the Imperial War Museum to Kew Gardens, the Science Museum Group and the Victoria and Albert Museum. The pilot runs to February 2027 with a stated goal of full commercial self-sufficiency by 2030 — a state-run clearinghouse for selling institutional archive access, the first concrete model of its kind seen so far for who actually buys this material and how.

2. The Royal Institute of British Architects is digitising a 4.6-million-item archive on commercial royalties, not government money.

Only about 150,000 of RIBA's drawings and photographs have been digitised so far; the institute says it's self-funding a push toward a million images by autumn 2027, timed to its 200th birthday, entirely through commercial royalty partnerships rather than any state grant. It's a concrete example, in architectural drawings specifically, of how slow and expensive institutional digitisation is without public funding — and a working business-model alternative to a subsidy.

3. A new paper finds the newest image-recognition models are worse than older ones at telling objects apart.

A same-day research paper identifies a "pareidolic" noise-pattern subspace inside the DINOv2 and DINOv3 vision models that eats up most of their feature variance and actively suppresses their ability to pick out individual objects in a scene — a problem the earlier DINO model doesn't share. Scaling that subspace down restores the newer models' ability to find objects, so the defect is a fixable property of how they were trained rather than a hard ceiling. This bears directly on which model a system doing visual search without labels should run on — telling one object apart from clutter around it is exactly the capability at stake.

4. Cooper Hewitt is building a 2027 exhibition entirely from a late designer's prototypes and working files.

The Smithsonian's design museum has acquired more than 50 objects from the estate of designer Tobias Wong — finished products, prototypes, digital design files and ephemera, given by his family, friends and collaborators — for a monographic exhibition planned for late 2027. It's a clean example of a named design museum treating unfinished, in-process material as worth collecting in its own right, not just the shipped product — and a useful timing marker: two years between the 2025 acquisition and the public show.

5. The Rijksmuseum confirms its "find similar artwork" search actually shipped.

The museum's own year-end release states plainly that it launched visual-similarity search across its entire Collection Online in early December — every artwork browsable by shape, colour, style and object type, no captions involved. Earlier coverage only had a vendor's technical account describing this as research still planned for 2025–2026; this is the museum itself confirming the feature is live, collection-wide — strong validation for search-by-visual-similarity as an alternative to labels and keywords.

Yesterday's deep dive

A deep-dive into who actually owns, may publish, and may delete digitisation capture files found that an earlier assumption — that no authority requires a capture vendor to delete its files — doesn't hold up. Both UK government model contracts and two of Japan's national digitisation tenders open this month require the vendor to eventually lose the files, usually after a cost-bearing retention period. In UK public contracts, ownership of what a supplier creates is a numbered option the buyer ticks before the tender even goes out, and the one heritage tender pack actually read takes everything by outright assignment. In Japan, the rights clause is published at every contract tier in three different forms, while the digitisation specification itself is gated behind bidder qualification at the national library, the national archives and the cultural-heritage research institute — and in both of this month's Agency for Cultural Affairs procurements, the specification says the agency holds the rights while the attached contract lets the contractor keep them instead. In every instrument read, the files go to whoever commissioned the work, never to the person whose material was captured. One vendor in this space, StudioNXT, publishes maker-owns terms and explicitly names unfinished work as worth keeping — the closest comparable found yet to the kind of ownership promise we're trying to build.
