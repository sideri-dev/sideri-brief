Today

Kyoto's own city planning office confirms machiya townhouses are still disappearing at about two a day, and just extended its preservation plan to a ten-year horizon running through 2035.

The city's public-comment leaflet and draft second-phase plan put the toll at exactly 5,566 machiya lost over eight years, against a surveyed base of just over 40,000 — and the new plan period runs fiscal 2026 through fiscal 2035, longer than the plan it replaces. One figure needs a caveat: a roughly ¥462 million project-cost number circulating in English coverage doesn't appear in any of the four Kyoto city documents checked directly, only in secondary reporting of the city's own budget summary. It's about as direct as evidence gets for architecturally significant buildings disappearing on a measurable, accelerating schedule, which is why it's the subject of today's deeper companion piece.

The international standards body behind cultural-heritage imaging quality has opened a new draft standard specifically for digitizing negatives, slides and transparencies.

ISO/WD 19264-2 extends the existing ISO 19264 imaging-quality method to transmissive originals, explicitly excluding microfilm and non-heritage materials like X-ray film. It's still a working draft — the public-comment period closed in mid-July — and surfaces so far on only one source, a standards-institute mirror rather than ISO's own site. Still, it's a concrete marker of what "properly measured, standards-conformant capture" is coming to mean for archival imaging.

India's Ministry of Culture has signed a memorandum with Google Arts & Culture to digitize national heritage, centered on a new national museum still under construction.

The partnership covers protected monuments, national museum collections and manuscript heritage, confirmed independently by both a wire report and India's own state broadcaster. It's a clean, current answer to a recurring question in this space — who actually pays for heritage digitization — and the answer here is a national government choosing a platform partnership over commissioning its own capture hardware.

A new museum-sourced dataset shows how much fine-tuning matters for reconstructing fragmented historical textiles from photographs.

Built from 18,644 Metropolitan Museum of Art textile images, a model trained generically only reassembles real degraded Viking-era fragments correctly about a quarter of the time. Fine-tuned specifically on that kind of damaged material, accuracy jumps to nearly two-thirds. It's a sharp, concrete data point on what it actually takes to make computer vision useful on fragmentary, degraded cultural material: the training data has to match the damage, not just the subject.

Singapore's heritage board has quietly dropped its bid to get the Padang recognized as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The board says it will no longer pursue the nomination — UNESCO's advisory bodies had asked for a larger protective buffer zone that would have burdened private building owners nearby — and is redirecting its attention toward intangible heritage listings instead. It's the third Singapore heritage story to surface in three days here, following Tuesday's bungalow-conservation story, worth flagging as a pattern: a national authority choosing targeted, lower-friction protection tools over a prestige designation, twice in a row.

Yesterday's deep-dive

Yesterday's deep-dive tested the Haji Kahar bungalow story from Tuesday's brief and found two things wrong with it: the building is two storeys, not five, and "no archival record found anywhere" isn't true — Singapore's heritage board described the site's outbuildings in print back in 2016, and the national archive holds dozens of photographs and two unexamined building-plan microfilms, just filed under the wrong street name. What's actually missing is a measured record and any public version of the planning authority's own conservation study, which drove the outcome but was never published. Singapore's law, as far as it could be read, mirrors the pattern seen elsewhere and sharpens it: the one mandatory pre-demolition survey covers the neighbouring buildings, not the building coming down, and no statute found puts a recording duty on the owner. Much of the authority's own website returned access errors throughout the research, so the underlying gazette notice and several core claims stay single-source and partly unverified.
