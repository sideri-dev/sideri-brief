Today

1. Google's own health-AI documentation shows how it runs similarity search over a 65,802-slide pathology archive using nothing but unsupervised clustering and plain distance math.

Each slide is broken into roughly 18,000 small image patches and embedded; clustering compresses that down to about 500 representative embeddings per slide; the resulting ~33 million vectors sit in a database indexed for cosine-similarity lookup, and a full-archive search runs in about 30 seconds. No manual labels appear anywhere in the pipeline — clustering shrinks the index, then plain distance math ranks it. It's close to the architecture we've been sketching for our own retrieval layer, so this is a genuine real-world precedent at scale.

2. The European Southern Observatory's ALMA Science Archive has quietly shipped a "morphological image similarity search" across 455,000+ astronomical images.

Pick one image and the system returns the 1,000 most visually similar, ranked by a self-supervised model trained to ignore rotation and scale — no caption or metadata field involved. It's actually been live about a year; the announcement page's own dates (published December 2025, updated this month) make clear it isn't the breaking news its web address implied. Carried anyway as the clearest working example yet of connecting images purely by visual distance, with interactive re-ranking built in.

3. Japan's Cabinet secretariat has published a five-year national digital-archiving strategy for 2026–2030 — and across 47 pages, it never once mentions image-similarity search, architectural drawings, or redevelopment-driven demolition.

The plan was decided in May 2025 by two renewed government archiving councils (not the Intellectual Property Strategy Headquarters, as secondary coverage had it — corrected here against the document's own cover page, after the original government link went dead and had to be tracked down on a different domain). It sets national targets for metadata standardization, digitization of national museum holdings, and open commercial licensing of cultural-property data. It reads as a metadata and licensing document, not a capture-method one — notable as much for what it skips as for what it covers.

4. The photo-management tool Excire Foto 2027 just extended its offline, subscription-free visual-similarity search to video.

The update pulls searchable frames out of each clip — by face, person, keyword or plain visual similarity — with no cloud upload involved; a matched frame opens the video at that exact moment or seeds a fresh similarity search. Everything runs locally on Windows or Mac, and the update is free for existing owners of the $249 one-time-purchase product. A shipping consumer product doing almost exactly this kind of search, priced the way we think about pricing: one-time, local, no subscription.

Yesterday's deep dive

A deep dive into a Japanese national archive's "item visualization map" found it wrong on scale and mechanism. The map holds only about 1.25 million points — roughly a quarter of the items that carry a thumbnail, and well under 4% of the full multi-million-record registry — with over a hundred source collections absent entirely. The national library names the underlying model in print as OpenAI's CLIP, with cluster labels auto-extracted from item titles; the only genuinely label-free part of the whole system is the placement of the dots themselves, and even that encoder has never been published. The same archive has in fact run keyword search, text-to-image and image-to-image search side by side for years. Across the wider field, these similarity-map features reliably outlast any published evaluation of whether they actually work.

This week

Three technical deep dives this week converged on one verdict: shortcuts that avoid fully re-building a visual-search index after swapping in a better image model buy only about half the quality benefit of a plain rebuild, and only pay off on archives far larger than ours — rebuilding from scratch is cheaper at our scale. The real hazard, newly measured, is a half-migrated index, where mixing old and new embeddings quietly makes results worse while both ends of the system look healthy.

Every national-scale "label-free" visual search that actually shipped this year turns out to lean on text somewhere underneath — a theme that recurred across multiple institutions this week. A separate finding on human similarity judgment explains why: people agree with each other only about two-thirds of the time on what counts as "similar," so no single automated measure can promise it matches what a given viewer meant.

This week's evidence also split the money question cleanly: capturing cultural and creative material is well funded almost everywhere — subsidies, grants, salvage budgets — but nothing obliges or pays anyone to make what's captured reachable afterward. A government audit found most municipalities keep excavated artifacts undisclosed, and even a state-run rights marketplace still can't publish a price for archive access.
