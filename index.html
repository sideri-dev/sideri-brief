Google paid $10 million for a dead airline's internal data — and that's now a pattern, not a one-off.

MIT Technology Review reports that wind-down specialist SimpleClosure has started selling failed startups' internal data — emails, chats, documents — and that Google outbid AI data broker Mercor to buy Spirit Airlines' decades of records for $10 million after the carrier collapsed. Bloomberg separately reports SpaceX's AI unit is weighing the same move. Lawyers quoted call the practice "legally radioactive"; one law professor argues workers should have had to consent before their employer sold their data at all. It's the clearest dated, dollar-figure case yet of what happens when a maker's own record becomes a saleable asset the moment the company holding it folds — exactly the scenario a local-only, no-cloud design is meant to make impossible.

Google published hard numbers on the embedding model behind MediaPipe's on-device retrieval.

The EmbeddingGemma 2 launch post states a 740-million-parameter footprint running on as little as ~191MB of RAM for text-only weights, or ~567MB for the full multimodal model, with a bundled demo storing embeddings in a local SQLite database and matching by cosine similarity in single-digit milliseconds. These are concrete, vendor-published memory figures for anyone building on-device retrieval around this model.

A new method stores one version of an image instead of a different one for every possible question asked of it.

Most visual-token compression ranks what to keep based on the question being asked — fine for a one-off query, bad for anything written once and read many times later. This arXiv paper keeps every token and only varies the detail, producing a single representation that serves any future query equally well, beating both uniform compression and question-aware pruning on two benchmarks. Single source for now.

The V&A acquired a reconstructed 2006 YouTube page — specifically because the code behind it is legible to future researchers.

The acquisition pairs the original front-end code (as the Internet Archive captured it in December 2006), a Flash emulator to run it, and the very first YouTube video. The museum's own stated reasoning: labelled comments in the code "enable future researchers, conservators and curators to understand the work undertaken and the logic behind it" — a conservation principle it wants applied across its collections, digital included.

Yesterday's deep-dive

An earlier deep-dive pinned down, with two independent sources for the first time, exactly where the Raspberry Pi 5's antenna sits on the board — and found that the official Active Cooler clears it by about a millimetre, a margin smaller than the measurement error itself. A HAT+ accessory board is a cleaner story: the spec sets no fixed board outline and never mentions the antenna, and a legacy-footprint HAT at the standard standoff height sits safely clear. The practical upshot: the sharper enclosure risk turned out to be the default cooling solution, not the stacked accessory board that had drawn more attention until now.
