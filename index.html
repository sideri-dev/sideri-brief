Today

Milwaukee just put a real price on capturing a building before it's torn down — and it's cheap.

Milwaukee's mayor signed an ordinance making it the first US city to require laser scanning before a historic building can be demolished. The city's own figures: around $800 for a small building and a few thousand dollars for a large one, with one scan — a historic office building — taking just 25 minutes. Neither the city nor local reporting says who actually pays. It's the first concrete price found anywhere for exactly the kind of capture-before-loss work this research is trying to understand the economics of.

Japan added Osaka Castle's keep and a three-times-relocated Meiji guesthouse to its national cultural-property list.

Japan's Cultural Affairs Agency designated eight buildings as newly Important Cultural Properties and upgraded one more — including Osaka Castle's 1931 reconstructed keep, funded by citizen donations, and 蔵春閣, a Meiji-era guesthouse that has physically moved three times and now sits in Niigata prefecture, independently confirmed by the prefecture's own page. Japan's national count of Important Cultural Properties is now 2,619, across 5,624 buildings. Routine government business, but exactly the volume and kind of provenance record this research is tracking.

A new paper finds duplicate objects in a real heritage archive from one reference photo per object and nothing else.

The arXiv preprint spots duplicate artefacts in an actual cultural-heritage repository using a single anchor image per object and no labeled negative examples, reaching an F1 score of 90.79. One unreproduced preprint, not an established result — but the closest academic proof yet that connecting images by visual similarity alone, with no labels or tags, works on a real archive rather than a clean lab benchmark.

Google quietly built image-based search over 65,000+ pathology slides that never looks at a diagnosis.

Google Research's own write-up describes nearest-neighbour search across more than 65,000 whole-slide pathology images, each reduced to a single 384-number embedding, with no diagnostic label used anywhere in the similarity calculation. The full archive — roughly 33 million embeddings — searches in about 30 seconds. Vendor-authored and single-source, but a real production system at real scale, with concrete numbers for how fast and how compact a no-label image-search system can run.

The Rijksmuseum confirms its no-label image search is live across its entire 800,000-artwork collection, not just a pilot.

The museum's own press release says the visual-search feature launched in December, letting visitors browse "by similarity in shape, colour, style and object type with just a click" across the whole collection. An earlier technical post from the museum's build partner described an 18,000-piece prototype built on the DINOv2 model that informed this launch — the production system isn't explicitly confirmed to be identical. Still, a major museum now runs no-label visual-similarity search live at full collection scale, not as a demo.

Yesterday's deep-dive

A closer look this week asked where Japan's demolition risk for architecturally significant buildings actually sits. On the one registry tracking both status and fate, 40 of 312 nationally selected modern buildings are gone — 39 of those 40 carried no legal protection at all, and none of the 19 buildings with the country's highest protected status has been lost. Nobody holds the drawings by enforceable duty: an architect's office keeps them only 15 years from the date each sheet was drawn, a builder's as-built copy lasts 10 years from handover, and the one municipal register outliving both is erased by law the moment the building itself is demolished. No statute anywhere requires a record before loss, and the only budget line found with that purpose is paid, case by case, by the owner at the moment of demolition. The gap is one of remit, not money: a building's image is demanded everywhere as proof of a transaction, never as a record of the thing itself.
