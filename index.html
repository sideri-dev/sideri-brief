Singapore's digital ministry just confirmed there's no special rule coming for camera-equipped wearables — the existing privacy law already covers them.

Responding to two parliamentary questions this year, Singapore's Ministry of Digital Development and Information stated plainly that no device-specific law exists for AI smart glasses or wearable cameras: the Personal Data Protection Act, the Penal Code, the Protection from Harassment Act and the Online Safety Act already apply, "regardless of… form factor." Crucially, the PDPA "generally does not require consent… for photography or recording in public spaces" — the baseline is permissive, not restrictive — while misuse of intimate or private content stays criminal under existing law, and sending data overseas needs comparable protection or the organisation risks PDPA fines. It's a clean, newly-published regulatory baseline for anyone building a camera-based capture device for the Singapore market.

Blackmagic just cut over $1,000 off two of its camera lines — and says it's simply passing on cheaper parts.

The Cinema Camera 6K drops from $2,715 to $1,595, and the Pyxis 6K line falls by roughly the same amount across all three mounts, with Blackmagic's own stated reason being "securing better pricing on parts" — not a tariff reversal, not a demand slump, just a straightforward pass-through. Five independent outlets confirm the same figures and rationale. It's a live example of a premium hardware maker choosing to hand a component-cost win straight to the customer rather than capturing it as margin.

Letterboxd, the film-logging app with no hardware at all, is reportedly up for sale at a $250 million valuation.

Sony Pictures is among the parties taking initial meetings, per TheWrap, with bankers floating $250 million against the roughly $50–60 million valuation a majority stake carried back in 2023 — a five-fold jump for a platform built entirely on people logging their own personal viewing history. The site passed 29 million members as of May. It's a clean data point for how big media buyers now value a pure software-and-community archive business built on individual record-keeping, independent of any device.

A Danish lighting house just launched a pendant lamp whose maker says the process may never be automated — and that's the point.

Carl Hansen & Søn's Begonya, designed by Øivind Slaatto, has each shade hand-assembled through 168 joining points over up to seven hours, in a material textured like handmade paper but hard to tear. Slaatto, on automating it: "it's an extremely hard process to automise… maybe it won't ever be automised, but if anyone can do it, it's Carl Hansen." It's a premium maker naming hand-labor-that-resists-automation as the actual sales pitch. Single-source for now; no second outlet has picked up the launch.
