An on-device sound classifier now runs at under a millijoule per inference.

MIT and Kioxia built a compact 158,144-parameter, 8-bit quantized CNN that runs entirely on an Analog Devices MAX78000 microcontroller and tells a rare Patagonian bumblebee apart from an invasive look-alike and background noise, at 86.1% accuracy in 10.4 milliseconds and 794 microjoules per classification. Both MIT's own writeup and the underlying Scientific Reports paper carry the identical figures. It's a clean existence proof of what a narrow, quantized, fully offline event classifier can cost in compute and energy on cheap hardware — a useful benchmark for anyone sizing a similar on-device classifier.

A new transistor design boosts a cheap thermal-imaging material's sensitivity fifteen-fold.

Yale's Xia Group paired a two-terminal transistor with standard microbolometer material and pushed its temperature-coefficient-of-resistance from about 10% to up to 150% per kelvin, without changing the sensing material itself. Uncooled microbolometers are the inexpensive, lower-accuracy end of thermal imaging, well behind cooled photon detectors and higher-end thermopile arrays on accuracy at real-world temperatures. If this materials advance reaches commercial parts, it could meaningfully close that accuracy gap — worth tracking for anyone comparing thermal sensor classes.

Logitech is being sued for keeping a $61 million tariff refund instead of passing it back to buyers.

The complaint alleges Logitech raised prices on over half its product line by up to 25% (14% average) in April 2025, citing tariffs, then collected the full refund by 30 June 2026 without lowering a single price — prices the suit says remain in place. It's a live cautionary case for any hardware brand that raised prices citing tariffs and now needs to decide what happens when those tariffs are refunded or reduced.

A thermal-plus-depth sensor fusion model hits gesture recognition at 50 milliwatts on a commodity microcontroller.

A preprint fuses an 8×8 thermal array with time-of-flight depth data into a 6,343-parameter model running on STM32 chips, at 92.3% accuracy and millisecond-level latency. It's a useful, quantified reference point for how small and low-power a two-sensor fusion classifier can get on a resource-constrained chip, even on a different platform and task. Single-source (preprint, not yet peer-reviewed).

Yesterday's deep-dives

One re-read the actual text of the EU's battery-removability law and found the corpus's understanding of it was already out of date: Article 11 has nine paragraphs, not eight, and its supporting Commission guidance was quietly revised on 14 July 2026 in a version not yet on the EU's own public legal database. The article sits entirely outside the EU's standard product-certification machinery — there's no certificate or approval process that can ever satisfy it, only after-the-fact market enforcement. A battery-bay design built around swappable commodity cells (rather than a sealed, purpose-built pack) turns out to gain nothing under this specific rule and adds real certification difficulty, while still holding value under several Asian markets' rules for an unrelated reason.

The other examined the same battery/enclosure design against US and EU wireless-certification rules. In the US, a chip module's existing radio approval typically only covers a "mobile" use classification evaluated at 20cm of separation from the body — a handheld or countertop instrument someone reaches for while working is probably the stricter "portable" classification instead, and the practical fix is a few centimeters of physical clearance built into the enclosure design from the start. In the EU, there turns out to be no equivalent certification-inheritance mechanism at all for a pre-certified radio module — a finding not stated anywhere else in the public record, confirmed here by a full-text search of the underlying law. Both point to the same lesson: some of these constraints are nearly free to design in now and very expensive to retrofit once an enclosure is finalized.
