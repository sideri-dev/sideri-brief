Today

A pretrained vision transformer's own attention turns out to separate individual objects in a frame with no labels at all.

A new preprint ("Objects as Addresses") finds that inside a frozen, already-trained ViT — including DINOv2 — a "query component" keeps object instances apart as a side effect of attention, usable for label-free grouping with no extra training. Clustering patches by it beats clustering final-layer features (.615 vs .289 on a standard metric) across eight pretrained models — relevant to a single process photo containing several objects.

A Scottish heritage body is spending £225,000 just to define a digitisation standard, not to buy any digitisation.

Historic Environment Scotland tendered the contract for an open, modular standard that smaller museums and archives can adopt incrementally regardless of size or budget — a government funding the rules before anyone sells the tool.

Kumamoto City now puts earthquake- and flood-damaged heritage restoration at over ¥76 billion, with no end date in sight.

Reported around the 2026 mayoral election, the figure covers cultural properties across the city — including the national Special Historic Site Kumamoto Castle ruins — damaged since the 2016 earthquake and subsequent record rainfall: a blunt scale anchor for what disaster-driven heritage restoration costs a city.

A CVPR 2026 paper narrows the gap between matching a line drawing and matching a photo, using frozen diffusion-model features rather than training something new.

The method ("SFA-DIFT") extends an existing sketch-photo benchmark and improves correspondence accuracy over prior best methods — modestly, under 2.2 points on average, real but not a breakthrough. It sits squarely on the sketch-to-photo matching problem a process archive needs to bridge different stages of the same work.

A deeper look at Milwaukee's laser-scanning preservation mandate shows just how narrow it really is.

The city issues roughly 250 demolition permits a year, but the ordinance requiring a scan before demolition applies to fewer than one case a year against at least 2,000 protected buildings — cheap for the city precisely because it almost never fires, a caution against sizing a heritage-capture offering on assumed demand.

Yesterday, revisited

An earlier deep-dive found the Nakano Sunplaza demolition story doesn't hold up as first reported: the rebuild was announced in 2018 on cost grounds, with redevelopment a written condition of a 2004 land acquisition, so the building's missing renovation-era drawings were never actually an input to that decision. The "drawings" story only entered the public record in February 2026, when an assembly member reported the mayor calling them "lost" — corrected by his own staff to "never handed over," distinct from the 1973 as-built drawings the ward does hold. What the missing records actually did: let the ward refuse a renovation-cost verification residents had demanded, since five consultancies said that survey couldn't be priced from as-built drawings alone. Tokyo separately confirmed its pre-1998 building-permit records were destroyed on schedule, closing off the 1997 renovation architects' identity for good. The case argues a continuous record doesn't keep a building standing — it keeps the decision about it open to checking, and this ward already says it wouldn't trust a recovered drawing anyway.

This week

No law anywhere this week's sweep touched — Japan, Singapore, partly the US — requires a record of a building before it's lost; the risk sits in the undesignated tier, since formally designated buildings almost never get demolished and already have a record regime. A record buys the decision staying open to checking, not survival — an accountability pitch to owners and residents, not a compliance pitch to regulators.

Every priced tier of the archiving market this week — Historic Environment Scotland, Ireland's national museum, a Japanese prefecture, Singapore's heritage study budget — bought recurring custody, tendered services or credentialed labour, never a one-time device; the only one-time price found anywhere was a $34.95 desktop app. The same week priced custody's downside: a court order was needed before a PBS station could reach its own archive at Iron Mountain, Third Light's customers reportedly pay $2,500 to retrieve their own files after a forced platform migration, and Iron Mountain posted record quarterly revenue.

Every technology win this week — a smaller search index, a fix for matching small objects in big images, a re-ranker that generalizes to new domains — improves retrieval on an index that stays frozen, but nothing in the literature answers adding new images without reshuffling what's already there. Every shipped product this week — a national museum, an attribute-extraction acquisition, a government choosing a platform partner — added labels back instead.
