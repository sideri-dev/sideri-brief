1. A new paper shows how to index a growing stream of screenshots in real time, with no OCR and no labels.

StreamEmbed notices most of a screen doesn't change between captures: it skips re-encoding any block that repeats its neighbor, and reuses cached keys and values for unchanged blocks, plus a small learned adapter. Against a 184-hour benchmark it runs up to 3.7x faster than encoding every frame whole, beats OCR-based indexing on accuracy at matched speed, and stores a simulated year of screen history in 0.13 GB of RAM. A concrete answer to the problem any on-device capture system has to solve: indexing a stream that never stops growing, with nothing hand-labeled.

2. The US federal government just put a number on what "archiving at scale" actually looks like: 21.7 million images.

The US Copyright Office has finished digitizing all 25,238 of its bound historical copyright record books — applications and paperwork from 1870 to 1977 — into a 21.7-million-image preview, ahead of folding it into its modern records system. A plain, government-stated scale figure, comparable to similar large-scale digitization efforts already underway at UCLA, Historic England and the Getty.

3. JSTOR just put a dollar figure on what "real" digital preservation costs a museum, as opposed to just hosting images.

JSTOR's own pricing page shows three museum tiers: basic access from $1,200/year, OAIS-compliant preservation from $5,000/year, and an AI-assisted tier (JSTOR Seeklight) from $6,000/year, scaling further by institution size. The jump from tier one to tier two is worth sitting with: actual preservation, not just a searchable image library, costs roughly four times bare sharing.

4. India just handed its national heritage digitisation project to Google, not to a hardware or services vendor, and didn't disclose what it's paying.

India's Culture Ministry signed an MoU with Google Arts & Culture to preserve and share the country's ancient manuscripts and monuments, including the collection going into the new Yuge Yugeen Bharat Museum in New Delhi. No budget was named, by the government or the broadcaster confirming it. It's the clearest evidence yet that the largest government heritage deals are won on platform-donation terms, not a paid commission — a free, platform-backed offer is the real competition for anyone pitching capture hardware to a government buyer, not a cheaper device.

5. A Spanish noble family just paid for a museum-grade 3D scan of a Goya portrait, and kept the data for themselves.

Factum Foundation, known for extremely high-fidelity cultural-heritage scanning, digitised Goya's portrait of the 13th Duchess of Alba in white for the Casa de Alba foundation, 3D and colour scanning it at the family's own palace. What stands out is custody: the record goes into the family's own archive as their painting's condition record, while Factum separately uses its own copy for a facsimile. A single-object commission where the client, not the capturing institution, ends up owning the record.

Yesterday's deep dive

Dug into a London exhibition billed as a living artist showing his own 25-year working archive as the exhibition itself, and found the premise doesn't survive the primary sources: what's on display is reference material — a commercial Disney World map and a map made by a current collaborator's studio — beside new prototypes built for this project, not a sketch, notebook or test recording from the artist's own past practice. Every account traces back to one sentence on the gallery's own page, which itself contains a factual error (a "2001... 40th anniversary" map for an anniversary that actually fell in 2011). The more interesting finding runs the other way: the artist's own dated public record thins as his practice grows — a daily log that stopped in 2013, a site that stopped posting news in 2019 — and his own separate archive project groups submitted sounds by nearest sonic neighbor with no labels at all. One case about a sound-and-motion practice a stills camera doesn't hold, supporting no general claim about what creators keep.
