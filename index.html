Today

Nakano Ward's own mayor just said, on the record, that the city demolished Nakano Sunplaza partly because nobody kept the drawings.

At his March 26 press conference, Nakano Ward Mayor Naoto Sakai said the city never inherited the renovation-era design drawings for the building — "we did not inherit the renovation construction drawings; we cannot grasp what was fixed where." A second city official added that no renovation-history documents came with the building at all when the city took it over from the former operator. Only the original as-built drawings from completion survive. It's about as direct a confirmation of our own archiving thesis as it gets — a named sitting mayor citing missing drawings as the stated cause of an irreversible demolition decision — and it's the subject of today's deeper companion piece.

Saitama Prefecture's new budget puts real yen figures on digitizing cultural property.

The FY2026 budget request funds 3D-modeling of 29 museum items at ¥14,809,000, plus digital publication and 3D-modeling of a further 503 cultural-property-storage items at ¥6,006,000. It's a useful data point below the national level on who actually pays for this kind of work and how much they spend. One honest note on our own process: this item initially reached us with no working source link and a mistaken claim that the budget document was encrypted — it wasn't, and we found and read the real document cleanly.

The National Museum of Ireland is paying €6 million just to sort and document its own archaeological collections.

The tender covers sorting, packing, documenting and preparing assemblages for accession and storage — no digitization or capture technology mentioned at all, just the labour of processing what the museum already holds. It's a reminder that a lot of institutional money in this space goes to services, not hardware or software.

Dai Nippon Printing and Japan's national archaeology research institute are building an AI tour guide for historic sites, not an archive.

Dai Nippon Printing and the Nara National Research Institute for Cultural Properties signed a research partnership in July aimed at a "next-generation site museum," targeting a conversational AI guide service by 2028. It's a clean example of a major incumbent choosing to monetize interpretation of heritage sites — an AI guide layered on top — rather than the underlying capture and recordkeeping work itself.

TOPPAN built a full digital twin of Himeji Castle in Unreal Engine 5, because the existing repair drawings weren't good enough to rebuild from.

The trigger was Kumamoto Castle's damage in the 2016 earthquake — Himeji City didn't want to be caught the same way, so it commissioned a point-cloud and drone capture (over 1,683 scan points, 30,000-plus photos from a DJI Mavic 3 Pro) and had TOPPAN turn it into a full real-time 3D model after a competitive proposal process. It's close to exactly the capture-before-disaster work we think matters here, with a city government footing the bill.
