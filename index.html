Today

A single-wholesaler closure forced a strainer maker to rebuild a lost weaving process from scratch.

Okawa Seiro-ten, a Takamatsu workshop, makes culinary strainers that require horsehair specifically — "horsehair only," in the maker's own words — and when the wholesaler supplying its loom components and horsehair closed without warning, the shop had to relocate an original loom and start re-sourcing horsehair from violin-string makers instead. Two independent Japanese outlets corroborate the account. It's the exact failure mode worth worrying about for any small-batch hardware maker: an invisible upstream supplier disappearing and forcing a reconstruction of process knowledge that was never written down. A 1940 stencil-cutting machine is still sold new, unchanged, for $2,400.

Core77 covers the Marsh Model H — built the same way for 86 years on case-hardened steel dies and zinc-alloy punches that hold their edge, no redesign and no digital layer ever added. It's a long-run precedent for treating a purely mechanical control surface — dials, buttons — as permanent industrial design, rather than something that ages against a software refresh cycle.

Ultrahuman's $70M Qualcomm round, updated: real revenue and subscription numbers now attached.

This funding round was covered previously; the same reporting, read again, carries figures the earlier coverage didn't — a $140M annual revenue run rate (up 45% year over year, projected to $200M by January), 800,000 rings sold (up from 700,000), and 12% of users paying for the optional PowerPlugs software tier. Yesterday's deep-dive

A deep-dive into Vermicular's latest price notice found it isn't the second move in ten weeks it first appeared to be — it's the fifth price action in four and a half years, and the notice template's middle clause is the same sentence the company used in 2023 to announce a price cut. The stated "rising material cost" doesn't hold for steel specifically — published price-index data puts steel below its early-2025 level, with the real pressure coming from non-ferrous metal, scrap, energy and timber instead. At roughly 3% the move reads as under-recovery rather than opportunism: the one traceable item rose to exactly the bottom of its own announced range, while a competitor raised over a hundred products 4–25% the same week. The real price defence turns out to be the brand's flat-fee lifetime re-enamelling program, whose discount against a new pan widens automatically with every list-price rise. Both 2026 notices were filed more quietly than any prior one — absent from the brand's own news page, picked up only by a trade outlet instead of the usual consumer press.
