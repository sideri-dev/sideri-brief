Meta's always-on smart glasses are producing real bystander-consent harm in India, days ahead of a sub-$105 rival.

Two documented cases: a Delhi protest video shot without consent went viral and triggered abuse against its transfeminine subject, and a public-interest litigation alleges Delhi police used Meta smart glasses to continuously film student demonstrators — including while they ate and rested. Tutorials circulate for defeating the recording-indicator LED, and India's voyeurism law offers only limited protection against covert filming in public. Meta's glasses retail at $420 there; Reliance Jio is readying a sub-$105 rival later this year. It's the sharpest live evidence yet of the bystander-harm mode that always-on capture hardware produces — directly relevant to how we think about capture scope and recording indicators on the device we're building.

Raspberry Pi's own blog just built a fully local, zero-cloud personal knowledge system.

The post runs Logseq over Syncthing on a Pi 500, a Pi Zero, and a Compute-Module-4-based Uconsole, because "it's a nice challenge to build a system that doesn't store your information on another person's computer." Retrieval is manual tag-and-search only — nothing ranks or recommends. It's a working proof, from the platform vendor itself, of the same local-first, no-ranking approach behind the archive we're building, on the same board family we rely on.

Raspberry Pi's own Official Magazine profiled a kid-built underwater camera on the exact camera module we're building on.

"Sensea," built by three makers aged 10 to 13, runs a Pi 3 and a Camera Module 3 in a sealed tube for about £130. Their design call: manual fixed focus over autofocus, "since autofocus was likely to struggle when encountering suspended particles," logging 10-second clips and timestamped temperature via a clock that keeps time with no internet connection. A small project, but vendor-published validation of the same instinct our own hardware depends on — fixed, unattended capture over live interaction, and a timestamp instead of in-the-moment selection.

Direct, dated evidence that professional creators will pay to have their workflow fused into one coherent system rather than piecing tools together themselves.

Yesterday's deep-dive

A verifier correction on Raspberry Pi Holdings' H1 2026 results turned out only half right: two claims it killed as unsupported are word-for-word in the analyst presentation filed the same day, which wasn't checked the first time. That presentation shows a key memory component's cost per gigabyte nearly quadrupled in six months, driving most of the inventory-value swing, while the company describes moving into a net debt position the following month and doubling its bank facility. The regulated filing and the investor presentation actually disagree on when supply eases — one says next fiscal year, the other says only two of four memory tiers ease by the following quarter. A large commitment figure already in circulation turns out to be an unrelated processor agreement, not memory stockpiling. On the retail shelf in Japan, the exact low-capacity board this build already relies on is now out of stock, and none of the newly-qualified second-source memory vendors cover the higher-capacity tier.
