Today

Brussels closed off the exemption route that might have applied to kitchen hardware — battery removability still stands.

The European Commission's delegated act adds six new exemption categories to the EU Batteries Regulation's user-removability rule — wearables, electric toys, ATEX-scope equipment — but even those still require an independent professional to be able to remove and replace the battery, not the end user. None of the six categories resembles a kitchen instrument. This confirms a user-replaceable battery design stays the compliant path for anyone selling into the EU; there's no shortcut available. Two independent sources confirm it, including the Commission's own page.

Bambu Lab opened a permanent showroom in Tokyo — a "certified store" embedded inside a distributor's own office.

Distributor Brule Inc. now runs a permanent, showroom-type space near Minowa Station where customers see 3D printers running and buy on the spot. It's modest by flagship standards — office-embedded, not standalone retail — but it's a real data point on how a design-forward hardware brand chooses to show up physically in Japan, worth weighing against any pop-up-only or online-only channel strategy.

A Japanese maker is selling an ESP32-family precision instrument direct-to-consumer, on a developer-freedom pitch.

Strato Vision's "ULSA EVO" ultrasonic wind-direction sensor pairs an STM32 for precision measurement with a user-programmable ESP32-C3, weighs 45 grams, and sells for ¥89,100 through the maker's own online store. It's a real domestic precedent for pitching instrument-class hardware direct-to-consumer in Japan, and a candidate reference for splitting precision control and user-programmability across two chips.

Tokyo's Artizon Museum opened its first design exhibition ever — an Ettore Sottsass retrospective, through October 4.

More than a hundred works across four chapters trace Sottsass from his Olivetti years through the Memphis movement, alongside pieces by Shiro Kuramata and Michele De Lucchi. This is the primary-source material behind Teenage Engineering's whole colorful, personality-forward design language — the originals, not the references, on view in Tokyo right now. MIT's own project page and MIT's own research paper disagree by 4.6x on the size of the same smell-classification dataset.

The project page describes 180,000 time steps over 50 hours of data; the paper behind the widely-cited 63.3% smell-classification accuracy figure describes 828,000 data points over 68 hours. Same team, same underlying work, two primary sources that don't match — which doesn't invalidate the accuracy figure but means it shouldn't be treated as a settled ceiling for gas-sensor-based smell classification until the discrepancy resolves.

Yesterday's deep-dives

One dive found that the newly-shipped stable ESP-IDF v6.1 firmware release's headline breaking changes are written against an older baseline than the one actually in use — so every flagged change, including a cryptographic-storage-format break, turns out to already be present in the currently pinned patch release. There's no cryptography migration required at all, because the chip in question doesn't even have the hardware peripheral the changed code targets. The real reason to move to the new release is a support-lifecycle deadline in March 2027, not anything actually breaking.

The other re-checked Japan's electrical-safety law against a user-replaceable-battery design and found it pulls no separate certification burden at all, provided the cell ships pre-installed. But the same research also found the domestic retail channel for commodity replacement battery cells in Japan is nearly empty — one hobby electronics shop stocks exactly one bare cell — and that a major Japanese battery manufacturer itself publicly disclaims the whole consumer single-cell replacement channel as unauthorized. That's a real challenge to any battery-bay design built around swappable, shop-bought commodity cells.
