Today

Japan's trade ministry wants to move power banks into a tougher, third-party-inspected certification tier after accident reports nearly quadrupled.

At an August 31 subcommittee meeting, METI laid out a plan to reclassify mobile batteries and power banks from self-certified "round PSE" to third-party-inspected "diamond PSE" — citing accident reports rising from 51 in 2021 to 192 in 2025. Enforcement is targeted for March 2027, with a one-year transition period.

For any hardware maker shipping into Japan, it's a reminder that battery-pack accessories specifically are being pulled toward a much stricter, costlier conformity track — worth watching before committing to any battery-pack accessory plan.

Teenage Engineering's own direct-store numbers show a rebound, not a recovery.

Third-party analytics on the company's direct storefront show revenue down 22% over the last three months. The more telling number sits one layer up: 2025 itself was already down more than 50% year-over-year, so the touted 50%+ growth projected for 2026 is a bounce off a much smaller base, not organic growth against a stable prior year. No methodology is disclosed, so treat the absolute figures as directional.

It's a useful reminder for anyone reading a design-led hardware brand's growth headline at face value — the base year matters as much as the percentage.

Balmuda's ¥59,400 sleep clock has shipped 5,000 units.

Cumulative domestic-plus-overseas shipments crossed 5,000 as of August, up from over 1,000 orders at the March pre-order opening.

It's a rare concrete volume figure for a Japanese instrument-positioned, direct-to-consumer product in the ¥50,000–60,000 range — useful context for anyone benchmarking premium kitchen-hardware demand in Japan.

A researcher demonstrated a full vision pipeline — training through inference — running entirely on a thumb-sized microcontroller.

The setup uses a $15-40 Seeed ESP32-S3 XIAO board with 8MB PSRAM, training three-class 64x64 image classification in about 9 minutes per run and running inference in real time at 6.3 frames per second, in roughly 1,750 lines of dependency-free C++ with no external machine-learning framework.

It's a working proof that a narrowly-scoped, on-device detection task — the kind of thing we're interested in for sensing physical events, not deep learning — is genuinely achievable on hardware one generation behind the chip we're building on.

A century-old Japanese enamelware maker opened its first-ever flagship store, built around watching the product get made.

Noda Horo, founded in 1934 and still the only Japanese enamelware maker running its entire manufacturing process in-house, opened a flagship shop with an attached kitchen and refrigerator so customers can immediately test container sizes and usability against real appliances.

It's a second concrete example of a single-category Japanese craft manufacturer choosing a hands-on, story-forward retail format over a conventional showroom — a useful data point for anyone weighing that kind of format for their own retail plans.

Yesterday's deep-dive

A distributor's "estimated restock date" for a newly launched microcontroller module turned out to be a calculated artifact, not real supply information — the site derives it from a fixed lead time plus the day you happen to load the page, and the apparent "slip" reported a day earlier was just calendar drift. Underneath that non-event, three findings held up: the chip's on-package memory is fixed per die rather than a separately configurable module option, with an 8MB floor across every documented variant; its machine-learning toolchain has actually supported the chip since May, correcting an earlier claim that support was only community-demonstrated rather than official; and its path to "stable" software support depends on a release that hasn't shipped yet and isn't expected until year-end. The chip's formal radio-certification status in Japan remains unfiled.
