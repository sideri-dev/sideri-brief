Today

Espressif's own blog spells out exactly how EU vulnerability reporting will work once Article 14 of the Cyber Resilience Act starts applying on September 11.

Filing runs through ENISA's Single Reporting Platform, web forms only at launch (no API), and only named "Assigned Representatives" — individuals with personal EU Login accounts and two-factor authentication — can submit on a manufacturer's behalf. Espressif is explicit that the reporting duty is tied to a manufacturer's own finished product: a flaw unreachable in the shipped device isn't the product maker's to report. This adds real operational detail — who files, how, and by when — to the broader CRA compliance picture for anyone shipping a connected product into the EU.

Panic refunded every tariff dollar it had charged Playdate customers, unprompted.

The company charged a 19% import surcharge, stopped charging it in April, and then on August 25 wrote every affected customer a check for the difference. "It's just not our money to keep," founder Cabel Sasser told PC Gamer, "that's an easy way to know you made the wrong call in the first place." It's a clean precedent for showing import duty as its own line at checkout rather than folding it silently into the sticker price.

Framework cut its LPCAMM2 memory prices and is automatically refunding everyone who already paid the higher rate.

Orders that already shipped at the old price get the difference back automatically; a subset of pending pre-orders gets the lower price outright, with no customer having to ask. It's a real-world instance of turning a component-cost drop into a customer refund rather than a quiet margin gain — a useful posture to watch as memory pricing keeps swinging through 2026.

Designer Noritaka Tatehana opens a 15-year handcraft retrospective at Tokyo's Joshibi Art Museum on September 10, staging his actual shoemaking tools next to the finished work.

MIT built 3D-printed objects that visually signal correct use with zero electronics, using lenticular lenses baked into the print itself.

A bottle cap screwed on properly shows green with a checkmark; loose, it flips to red with an exclamation point — purely from how light bends off a lens layer at different viewing angles, no sensor or circuit involved. It's a striking example of a mechanical, electronics-free state-indicator technique: a way to give an object a tactile, physical confirmation signal without adding any firmware or component cost.
