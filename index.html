Raspberry Pi's own blog just documented a concrete hardware path from "demo" to always-on local vision triggering on the Raspberry Pi 5.

A third-party add-on board, Sixfab's AI HAT+, built around DEEPX's DX-M1M chip, adds 25 TOPS of dedicated AI acceleration at roughly 3 watts, while leaving the Pi 5's own CPU completely free for camera handling, application logic, connectivity and device control. The posted benchmarks are concrete: 2,361 frames per second on a lightweight image classifier, 155 fps on a heavier segmentation model, and a small language model answering in under 600 milliseconds. This is Raspberry Pi's own documentation, not a rumor — a real, dated answer for how continuous camera-triggered capture can run entirely on-device, with zero cloud inference, on hardware already shipping today.

A new benchmark puts a hard number on how far even the best AI models are from matching a human watching continuous first-person video.

The S-EMBER benchmark built 388 hours of real first-person footage, captured on smart glasses, into 9,448 question-answer pairs, each requiring a model to both answer correctly and pinpoint exactly when in the footage the answer came from. The strongest model tested manages both at once at less than half the rate a human does. It's a sobering, directly measured ceiling on exactly the kind of query that on-device retrieval systems over continuous personal video are being built to answer.

It's a rare, current, first-party data point for whether a niche creative-archive tool can sustain itself on subscriptions alone without chasing venture-scale growth.

The Design Museum London just built an entire flagship exhibition around a working creator's raw process archive, not her finished work.

Set designer Es Devlin's new show, "Other Worlds," open now through April 2027, centers on a detailed replica of her actual studio — her books, maquettes and objects — plus a dedicated wall installation of thirty years of sketches, diaries and prints going back to her schooldays. It's the sharpest version yet of a pattern worth watching: major design institutions choosing to exhibit a maker's unfiltered process record rather than only the polished output.

A materials-science paper puts a real number on how much a self-healing coating can protect electronics from the kind of punishment an always-on device takes over years.

A liquid-metal-coordination polymer restores over 90% of its original mechanical properties after damage, and in direct CPU-cooling tests kept a chip running about 20°C cooler than a standard non-healing thermal paste after 500 cycles of extreme thermal shock (−10°C to 100°C, 16 hours accumulated). For any fanless, passively-cooled device meant to run unattended for years, that's a concrete answer to a real degradation risk. One caveat: this is a single academic source, and a submitted cycle-count figure needed correcting against the paper's own numbers — the corrected 20°C/90%+ headline figures hold up.

Yesterday's deep-dive

An earlier deep-dive found that a widely-reported six-CVE Electron security batch was substantially mis-stated. The actual April 2026 batch is 18 CVEs, not six, and exactly one — not six — concerns offscreen rendering; its real severity score is 8.1, not the 9.2 first reported, a figure that appears in no primary source at all. More importantly, the underlying concern doesn't apply: Electron's "offscreen rendering" is an unrelated, opt-in bitmap-export feature, not the web-standard canvas API an infinite-canvas rendering approach would actually use, and the advisory itself states plainly that apps which don't use offscreen rendering or deny child windows aren't affected. The one genuinely useful finding the original report missed: a second Electron CVE batch from late September, where the fixes had again shipped 29–44 days before the public disclosure — the real, recurring lesson being that a vulnerability feed on this framework structurally reports holes that were already closed.
