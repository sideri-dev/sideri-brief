Bosch and Espressif just shipped ready-made dev boards pairing an environmental gas sensor with a radio chip.

Espressif and Bosch Sensortec jointly released two ESP32-C5-based platforms: ESP-SensairShuttle, a modular evaluation board with swappable modules for the BME690 gas sensor plus an IMU and magnetometer, and ESP-Spot, an open maker board built around the motion sensors. Availability is worldwide except sanctioned regions, confirmed on both companies' own press pages plus five independent trade outlets. The BME690 is the newer, lower-power successor to the BME688 gas sensor already used in some cooking- and air-quality-sensing designs — this reference board is off-the-shelf bring-up hardware for anyone evaluating it.

A published ECG paper doubles as a real-hardware sizing benchmark for small on-device anomaly detectors.

The paper runs an INT8-quantized autoencoder — a compact 180KB model, not a bulky labeled classifier — on an ESP32-S3 microcontroller running TensorFlow Lite Micro, hitting 84% recall and 9-millisecond inference across more than 95,000 real ECG segments. Different domain (cardiac signals, not cooking), same microcontroller and inference toolchain increasingly common in small connected-hardware designs. It's a single preprint without independent replication yet, but its own published figures check out exactly.

Vermicular launched its first-ever kitchen knife, at ¥34,980, with a named steel partner.

The Nagoya-based cast-iron cookware maker's blade material, called VPS, was co-developed with a named partner, Nagoya Special Steel, and the handle is hollow-cast enamel matching the brand's existing cookware colorways. Three independent sources confirm the price, the partner's name, and the June 3 release date. It's a clean example of how a Japanese premium kitchen brand builds a craft narrative — naming the specific material supplier — around a single new product.

Yesterday's deep-dive — Japan's new import rules for direct-to-consumer sellers

An amended Japanese product-safety law package, in force since December 2025 with no grace period, makes any overseas business shipping directly to Japanese consumers a "specified importer" — triggering a mandatory 30-day filing and a paid, Japanese-speaking domestic representative, plus the overseas seller's name printed next to the safety mark. All of that disappears if a Japan-domiciled entity does the importing instead, which is the structure several comparable hardware and lifestyle brands' Japan storefronts already use. A typical kitchen appliance itself can sit outside this regime entirely — the trigger is usually what else ships in the box, such as a power adapter or a spare battery pack. The open question for any small overseas maker selling direct into Japan is purely who imports: a locally registered entity, an overseas entity with a contracted domestic representative, or a domestic merchant-of-record partner, each trading off speed, cost, and control differently.
