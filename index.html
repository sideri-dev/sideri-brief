Japan just created a new compliance category for overseas sellers who ship straight to Japanese consumers — and it may reach a maker's own online store, not just marketplaces.

Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI) amended package of four product-safety laws took effect December 25, 2025, creating a "特定輸入事業者" (specified importer) status: any overseas business selling PS-marked products directly to a Japanese consumer must appoint a Japan-resident, Japanese-speaking domestic responsible person and file a notification, plus meet technical-standard and inspection-record duties. The law's own wording leaves open whether it reaches a seller's own storefront the same way it reaches a marketplace listing — three sources were checked and none resolve it. For any hardware maker planning a direct-to-consumer launch into Japan, this is a distinct compliance gate from the PSE electrical-safety charger standard and from the 技適 radio-certification regime.

The EU's Right to Repair deadline has come and gone, and most of the bloc still isn't compliant.

Directive (EU) 2024/1799 required member states to transpose and apply it from July 31, 2026; as of July 30, "only a handful" had formally notified Brussels they'd finished. Today's covered-products list (Annex II) is washing machines, dishwashers, fridges, electronic displays, servers, phones and tablets, tumble dryers, local space heaters, and e-bike/e-scooter batteries from 2027 — no kitchen appliances yet. Erratum: an earlier read of this claimed the Commission was "preparing to extend its scope" over "2025–2030" — that specific claim is unfindable on the Commission's own page and is now dropped; the page only says the covered-products list gets updated as new EU repairability rules land, with no date range given. Worth tracking for repairable-battery and detachable-charger product designs generally, and for whether a kitchen appliance category is ever swept in.

A Japanese cookware brand just shipped a repair philosophy worth studying — at a premium price, with zero spec-sheet framing.

ADDI Designstudio's STEKA sauté pan, made for the Japanese brand Mozambique, is hand-cast from solid 5mm aluminum at a century-old Japanese foundry, ten pans a day, and its handle attaches with a single stainless screw instead of a rivet specifically so it can be replaced without discarding the pan. It ships in September at $240 ($192 on early reservation), with a five-year warranty. The brand's own language — "reduced, not visually, but structurally" — is close to a template for pitching a product as an instrument rather than a gadget, and it's live proof that visible, easy field-repairability sells at a premium in this exact market.

Google just opened its first store outside the US — in Tokyo's Omotesando district.

The Google Store Omotesando opened August 13 in Tokyu Plaza's "Omokado," Google's 11th store globally and its largest. It lands as the Pixel phone has quietly become Japan's #2 smartphone brand by share (roughly 11%, passing Samsung in February) behind Apple's roughly 60%. A comparable global hardware brand betting its first non-US flagship on this exact district is a notable signal for anyone weighing a showroom-first retail strategy in Japan.

Espressif shipped an official Linux developer preview for its newer ESP32-S31 chip — real, but explicitly not a production signal.

The preview (Buildroot 2025.02, Linux 6.18 kernel) carries Espressif's own caveat: "not recommended for production, and only targets the ESP32-S31," with irregular updates and no guaranteed stability. This confirms rather than changes an existing read on the chip: real ecosystem movement worth watching as a future migration candidate, but it still has no certified radio module, no Japanese radio-law certification record, and no production-grade software toolchain.

Yesterday's deep-dive — where does premium hardware actually sell in Japan

An earlier deep-dive looked at where a premium instrument-class hardware device could realistically sell in Japan. Finding: it doesn't fit the country's open retail funnels at all — the big lifestyle-goods chains cap out around ¥20,000 with no small-appliance category, and every documented small-maker deal at that tier pays roughly half of retail with a wholesale layer in between. The doors where higher-end design hardware actually lives — a major museum design store, department-store floors, curated lifestyle retailers — are entered by curatorial gate or buyer scouting, never an application form, and department-store consignment prices its commission by how fast an item sells, so a slow-selling newcomer pays the worst rate. The one format that fits a small, direct-to-consumer launch is a transaction-retaining showroom slot, where the maker keeps all the revenue from anything sold and the flat fee costs roughly the margin of a couple of units a month. Wholesale is worth taking on only as legitimacy, one door at a time — a major US design-museum store stood out as the one door whose reputational value plausibly justifies the roughly 50% cut it takes. The read: lead with showroom slots, treat a museum-store submission as a later move, and treat everything else as a target to scout rather than a plan to execute now.
