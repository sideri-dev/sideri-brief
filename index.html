Yesterday's deep-dives

One dive re-read the actual text of the EU's battery-removability law and found the working understanding of it was already stale — the article has nine paragraphs, not eight, and its supporting guidance was quietly revised in July in a version not yet on the EU's own public legal database. It sits entirely outside the EU's standard product-certification machinery, so there's no certificate or approval process that can ever satisfy it, only after-the-fact market enforcement. A battery-bay design built around swappable, off-the-shelf commodity cells turns out to gain nothing under this specific rule and adds real certification difficulty, while still holding value under several Asian markets' rules for an unrelated reason.

The other checked the same battery and enclosure design against US and EU wireless-certification rules. In the US, a chip module's existing radio approval typically covers only a "mobile" use classification evaluated at 20cm of separation from the body — a handheld or countertop instrument someone reaches for while working is probably the stricter "portable" classification instead, fixable with a few centimeters of designed-in clearance from the start. The EU turns out to have no equivalent certification-inheritance mechanism at all for a pre-certified radio module — a finding not stated anywhere else in the public record. Both point to the same lesson: some of these constraints are nearly free to design in now and very expensive to retrofit once an enclosure is finalized.
