1. A new method lets a visual-search system adopt a better image encoder without re-encoding its entire existing archive.

RELAY trains a new query encoder against the frozen original image gallery, then trains the new document encoder against both, so one query vector can score old and new encodings alike. Across three text-to-image benchmarks it recovers 8.66–10.23 points of Recall@1 versus the original systems and, with an optional refinement step, lands within a point of rebuilding the archive from scratch. It's a practical answer to a real constraint on any growing visual-search archive: when a stronger image-recognition model ships, the stored collection doesn't have to be recomputed wholesale to benefit from it. The paper tests text-to-image retrieval rather than pure image-to-image, so whether the same trick holds for a pure image-similarity archive is still open.

2. A pathology-archive retrieval method shows how to keep a growing, unlabeled image index current without rebuilding it.

CLEAR-WSI refines its candidate matches using a predicted or confirmed diagnostic label, then simply appends newly processed cases to an expanding memory bank rather than recomputing the whole index — beating the strongest baselines on four separate retrieval tasks by 2–3.75 points of precision. The domain is histopathology slides, not studio photography, but the structural problem it solves is a general one: a largely unlabeled archive that keeps growing, where every new item has to slot in without reindexing everything that came before it.

3. An architect's own kept drawings, not any institutional archive, are what let Okinawa rebuild Shuri Castle's main hall after its 2019 fire.

The reconstruction began by digitizing hand-drawn plans and notes that the original design-and-supervision lead had personally preserved — described in the source headline as a serial note-taker whose private notebooks and photographs enabled an early, faithful restoration of the Registered National Treasure. It's a clean, named example of process material surviving entirely outside any institution's own record, and of what that survival turned out to be worth once the building itself was lost. The exact date the rebuilt hall opened to the public isn't independently confirmed in this source (a sister article from the same publisher states only "seven years since the fire"), so that detail should be treated as unconfirmed.

4. The Cleveland Museum of Art's relaunched gallery includes a tool that finds similar pots purely by appearance, no captions involved.

Among 21 new interactive experiences spanning 10,000 square feet, a "Pottery Explorer" reveals collection pieces "based on visual similarity" as visitors work, and a separate 24-foot wall generates real-time AI transitions between artworks — with all models running locally inside the museum. It's a named, dated, shipped example of visual objects connecting to each other purely by how they look, with no keyword search involved, arrived at independently by a major museum.

5. A forensics researcher argues that proving an image is real has to start at the camera, not after the fact.

Writing in IEEE Spectrum, Hany Farid makes the case for content credentials embedded at the moment of capture: C2PA tools attach a cryptographically signed manifest to a photo the instant it's taken, Google's SynthID embeds an imperceptible signal directly into pixels, and on newer iPhones the camera sensor itself can cryptographically sign raw pixel data as it's captured. It's a broader, independent case for the same architectural move Apple has already shipped in its own hardware — evidence for whether a capture device should sign what it records right at the sensor, rather than only storing it afterward.

Yesterday's deep dive

A deep-dive into the UK government's new Creative Content Exchange — a marketplace meant to sell AI companies licensed access to museum and archive collections — found the picture is far less mature than the announcement suggested. The Natural History Museum's roughly £500,000 purchase bought a white-label contracting-and-billing platform from a vendor with no named heritage customers, not a search or imaging system; money flows backward from a marketplace, with the funder paying institutions up to £125,000 each just to list their collections. The live catalogue publishes no prices, most listed collections are "available on enquiry," and roughly three-quarters of the headline asset count arrived only the day before from a newspaper publisher's genealogy databases. No museum anywhere discloses a price for AI-training use, no participating institution reports AI licensing income, and every comparable precedent found — Europeana, Art UK, Norway's state agreement, Japan's national library — runs on subsidy or gives the material away for free. It's evidence against a tidy answer to who actually buys heritage-archive access: here it's grant bodies and one cost-absorbing commercial licensee, not licensing revenue.
