Today

An open-source smart-glasses platform just published the power-gating architecture an always-on capture problem needs — a coordinator chip stays awake so the vision processor can stay asleep, and it ran 11.5 hours on a 200 mAh battery.

The paper, called OpenGlass, builds a swappable event-camera/frame-camera rig around a Nordic nRF5340 coordinator chip that keeps a heavier RISC-V vision chip (GAP9, from Swiss chipmaker GreenWaves) powered down until something triggers it, then wakes it for inference. On a gesture-recognition benchmark the system hit 83.94% cross-subject accuracy at 78.3ms latency. It's a single preprint with no secondary coverage yet, but the power-gating architecture is directly relevant to any battery-powered always-on capture device, even running on different hardware.

A second preprint, "Gaze Archive," triggers capture from eye gaze rather than touch or motion, then retrieves by natural-language query — and it explicitly ranks results by inferred intent.

It's the fourth paper in about two months taking that position in this space — earlier ones scored relevance by inferred question-relevance or built salience-weighted personal-memory archives. Gaze as a capture trigger is a genuinely new signal in this line of research. Worth watching as a recurring design pattern across the field, not an isolated paper.

Giuseppe Penone's first Middle East exhibition opens Tuesday at the Qatar National Museum, built on more than 50 years of his own studies, sketches and photographs rather than finished sculpture alone.

Independently confirmed by Japanese design press (AXIS) and Qatar's own national news agency — two genuinely separate sources — with the museum's Old Palace and courtyard specifically given over to models, drawings and photographs rather than the sculptures themselves. It's a clean example of an artist's own process record becoming museum-grade material in its own right, curated by Hans Ulrich Obrist and Issa Al Shirawi.

Yesterday's deep dive

A deep read of GoPro's merger filing with a shell company called Starman Optical found the deal's own headline framing wrong in nearly every detail: Starman Optical is actually the entity that disappears at closing, not the buyer, and the real acquirer is a different newly formed shell backed by a New York real-estate family's capped equity commitment. The optics business the original announcement advertised turns out not to be a party to the deal at all. What does hold up, in GoPro's own filed words: tariffs and a 2026 memory-chip shortage forced price hikes that collapsed consumer demand, and a separate, previously untracked exposure — supply allocation, not price — stranded a $39.6 million purchase commitment. The filing contradicts itself in at least eleven places, and its own fairness opinion wouldn't value the company as a going concern. One contradiction worth watching: GoPro's cloud-storage contract terms promise to delete a customer's content on termination, while the company absorbing GoPro's user base is growing its own paid cloud-storage tier by more than 124% — a tension the filing leaves open.
