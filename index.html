Yesterday's deep-dive

A widely-reported "chip-substrate shortage" behind the Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W doesn't survive contact with primary sources: the word "substrate" appears zero times in Raspberry Pi's own H1 filing, which instead names "production congestion at the Taiwanese-based packager," with Zero sales down 9% and 1 million units on back order, and an engineer writing plainly that buyers ordering now "would not expect to see it this year." The "second substrate supplier by second-half 2026" claim traces back through a chain of paraphrase to a Reddit AMA that never said it. Two of the original claim's three price figures were also wrong. The deep-dive reframes the underlying question entirely: since the current camera module's sensor support lives in Raspberry Pi's own firmware rather than the board, the real fallback question is which Raspberry Pi board, not which single-board computer generally — and for a near-term build window, the smaller board isn't a live option regardless of the mechanism behind its shortage.

This week

Every layer of the Raspberry Pi platform moved this week, each surfacing through a different document than a watcher would normally check. A board shortage surfaced in a regulated financial filing, a camera sensor's component change surfaced in a product-change notice, a storage-corruption bug surfaced on a kernel mailing list with no formal vulnerability tracking number, and a wireless registration for the smaller board sits in a certification agent's name rather than the vendor's own. The build's fallback-board plan is now being settled by supply conditions rather than by the comparison originally planned.

Apple's newly shipped camera feature and an independent failure-mode analysis of the Raspberry Pi 5 arrived, separately, at the same design conclusion: cryptographically sign captured image data at the sensor buffer, before any storage round-trip. Without that, a storage-level read bug can produce a technically valid signature over bytes that were never actually captured. Signing at the buffer does double duty for a hardware-capture project — it proves origin and also gives a way to periodically verify stored data hasn't silently corrupted.

A cluster of design-world stories celebrating "process" as valuable split into two claims, and only one holds up under scrutiny. The claim that the trial-and-error before a finished work has value in itself is well supported by a current Tokyo design festival's own framing. The claim that documenting a process can help revive a technique once it's lost is not — no verified historical case was found anywhere of a filmed or recorded process actually recovering a technique that had died out. The honest framing is that a continuous record witnesses a process, not that it preserves one.
