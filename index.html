Plaud's Tokyo press event reveals a second product, and its newest premium model explicitly leaves Japan out

Plaud held a press event in Tokyo on October 21 unveiling "Plaud One," an eSIM-equipped always-connected AI wearable planned for release within 2026. The same event also showed a second, previously unreported product: "Plaud Note Pro Flow Edition," a card-type AI voice recorder with a higher-precision microphone than the existing Plaud Note Pro. Separately, an independent outlet reports that Plaud's Explorer Edition — limited to 2,000 units worldwide at $249.99 — explicitly excludes Japan from its sales regions. Taken together, that's a company in this ambient-capture-wearable category making its first real Tokyo move while deliberately holding a premium model back from the same market — a live data point on how a well-resourced competitor is sequencing a Japan launch, which is exactly the kind of question worth digging into further.

A new research paper cuts always-on smart-glasses perception cost by 27.5x in memory and 24.3x in energy

A preprint called EPIC describes an algorithm-and-hardware co-design for egocentric vision — the kind of constant, first-person perception a camera-equipped wearable runs — that filters what it looks at before processing it, instead of running every single frame through a full pipeline. Against a full-video-processing baseline, the authors report the approach "reduces memory footprint by 27.5× and energy consumption by 24.3× on average." It's a concrete architectural answer to one of the hardest problems in this category: keeping an always-on camera device inside a workable power and memory budget without constant full-pipeline processing. It's a single preprint with no outside press coverage yet, so treat the figures as the authors' own claim rather than independently confirmed.

A Tokyo material-research house opens a public lab that treats the making process as the exhibit, not just the material

DAIKEI MILLS is opening "MM (Material Matters)" on October 31 as part of SKAC EXTENSION 2026, running through the end of January 2027. It's described as a material-research institution that visualizes not just the material itself but the production process behind it, working alongside architects, designers, artists and manufacturers to explore new possibilities. It's a funded, physical bet that a maker's process — not just the finished object — is worth putting on public display, though it's worth noting this is a material library staging a process rather than one individual maker's own personal archive.

A grandson 3D-scanned his late grandfather's sculptures into a new line of lamps

Vincenzo Carmenati's sculptures became the raw material for a new lamp line designed by his grandson, Nicolau Carmenati, after being 3D-scanned — "a parallel edition that could have been made by him in another life," in Nicolau's words. The lamps, named "Oblò" (Italian for "windows"), were first shown at Feria Hábitat Valencia's "Nude" section in September. It's a striking, human-scale example of a maker's physical work being captured into a durable record that outlives the original act and becomes the starting material for something new, rather than simply disappearing once the act is over.

A premium Swiss furniture brand makes repairability the design thesis itself, not an afterthought

Embru's new "Patio Chair," designed by Moritz Schmid, is built on a modular construction explicitly following a "Design for Repair" approach, with individual components meant to be replaced or repaired as needed rather than the whole piece discarded. This is the manufacturer's own framing, without an independent teardown to confirm it, and repairability-as-design-thesis is not a new idea — but it's a useful real-world precedent for premium hardware that leads with serviceability as a selling point rather than hiding it.

Yesterday's deep dive

An earlier deep-dive took apart a Raspberry Pi manufacturing change notice and found it doesn't actually describe a change that reaches a buyer of the finished camera module — it only relieves manufacturers who buy the bare image sensor and build their own camera board around it. The investigation did settle something real along the way: the standard software stack for this camera never reads the memory chip in question at all, so a blank version of it only narrows the macro-focus range slightly, not focus overall. It also surfaced something unrelated but more consequential: a newer notice moves the committed Raspberry Pi 5 board to a new hardware revision with a different Ethernet networking chip, under a firmware-version requirement.
