Today

ESP32-S31 modules are finally listing on DigiKey, in two configurations — but stock sits at zero and the restock estimate just slipped further.

Both the WROOM-1 and a new WROOM-3 variant now show up on DigiKey's own listings, each specified at 16MB flash and 16MB PSRAM — the first concrete memory configuration numbers published for this chip anywhere. Zero units are in stock on either, and the estimated restock date moved from January 27 to February 1, 2027.

For anyone building on this chip family, a distributor listing existing at all is progress; zero stock and a slipping date are not. It's a small, telling snapshot of how tight memory-chip supply still is heading into 2027.

Nakagawa Masashichi Shoten is converting a Tokyo Station apparel-only shop into a full craft-lifestyle store, and opening a second new location the same week.

The craft retailer opens its first Kitakyushu store on September 10, at Amu Plaza Kokura, and follows on September 16 with a relaunch of its Tokyo Station "Grandsta" location — dropping the apparel-only format in favor of a full food/living/apparel lineup under the same brand name.

It's a live, dated example of a Japanese craft-lifestyle retailer betting a flagship transit-hub location on breadth of format rather than narrow specialization.

VAIO is raising prices across its entire lineup on September 18, citing AI-driven demand for memory chips.

VAIO's own announcement names the global spread of generative AI as the reason memory-semiconductor demand — and the component costs that follow from it — remain elevated. No percentage is disclosed; the new prices post the same day the change takes effect.

It's another dated, named instance of the DRAM/NAND cost shock reaching a premium Japanese hardware maker, on top of the wave already hitting game consoles and PC makers earlier this year.

Vermicular now charges ¥3,300 to re-sharpen the kitchen knife it sells — free if you bring it to a flagship store.

The paid service, running since August 1, takes 2–3 weeks and adds ¥550 or ¥1,100 in surcharges depending on how chipped the blade is; walking into a flagship store gets the same service free.

It's a clean example of an instrument-class kitchen brand converting a one-time hardware sale into a recurring paid relationship that also reinforces its craft story.

Framework's own blog is telling buyers not to default to the largest memory configuration for local AI.

The post argues for sizing memory to the actual workload rather than assuming more is always better, while still calling its 128GB tier "one of the best options" rather than the only reasonable one. No per-tier prices are given.

It's a useful primary-source example of how a premium, repair-forward hardware brand talks publicly to consumers about a memory-tier tradeoff — a genuinely hard question for any small-batch hardware maker right now.

Yesterday's deep-dive

A newly announced Heimann thermal-sensor part that looked like it might fill a spec gap turned out to have no real specifications at all — it's pre-launch marketing with no assigned part number, and even a year later the datasheet still carries placeholder values with no distributor stocking it anywhere. The more useful finding was technical rather than about that one part: the Melexis MLX90640 thermal sensor is limited to roughly 8 frames per second on the current generation of Espressif's ESP32 microcontroller line, because the chip's I²C bus interface tops out well below what the sensor's faster modes need. Separately, the same research closed a long-open question on Japan's export-control text: Japan's own regulation carves out the identical sensor-element-count threshold already used internationally, reversing an earlier reading that a sensor this size might face export restriction.
