Today

MediaPipe, Google's on-device machine-learning library, just added local embedding and vector-store retrieval alongside the hand-tracking features it already offered.

Google's own developer documentation confirms the release bundles a local vector database (SQLite under the hood) and an embedding model called EmbeddingGemma V2, with everything running on-device and no cloud round-trip required. That combination — on-device hand/occlusion signal detection plus on-device embedding and search in the same library — is useful evidence for anyone trying to build a camera device that decides what to capture and later retrieve without a network connection. The exact release date is unconfirmed (a sandbox-side access block on the GitHub release page itself), so only "shipped, live, as of early October" is verified.

A kitchen-tool shop in Tokyo's Kappabashi district is selling a cutting board made from a 200-year-old tree that stood at a Shinto shrine, numbered and hand-finished, going on sale next week.

The shop and a forestry company felled the torreya (kaya) tree only because an adjacent cedar risked falling onto it, then turned the salvaged wood into boards priced ¥9,900 and ¥33,000, finished with a thin "super-finish" surface shave and framed explicitly as a once-in-a-lifetime encounter with the material. It's a dated, concrete example of a craft seller building a product's value around the story and process behind the material, rather than the object alone.

Yesterday's deep-dive

An earlier deep-dive took apart a hardware vendor's own blog demonstration of a tiny on-device language model triggering a camera capture in about 76 milliseconds, and found the number measures something narrower than it looks: it's the cost of a model choosing the words "take a photo" from a text prompt, not the cost of actually taking one. More significantly, the demonstrated mechanism can't coexist with an always-watching, resident trigger process of the kind a passive-capture device would need — the camera software's exclusive-lock behavior means a background trigger and a one-shot capture call fight over the same resource, and one of them fails outright. The constructive finding: at a slow sampling rate, even a 76-millisecond check is cheap on almost every frame, so the variable that matters most for a battery- and heat-constrained device isn't which model is chosen, but how often it bothers to look at all.

This week

A device's enclosure is one coupled engineering budget, not three separate problems. Four unrelated investigations this week — into an accelerator add-on board, a function-calling language model, a carrier-board design and an antenna — independently landed on the same finding: the usual fix for heat inside a small sealed enclosure (a metal heatsink, a blower fan) sits exactly where a documented radio-antenna failure happens, and the only route to an external antenna needs the same expansion slot that storage and any accelerator hardware also want. Nobody had stated that chain explicitly before; the cheapest lever turns out to sit at the very top of it — how often such a device bothers to look at all, which sets the heat load, which decides whether a fan and metal are needed in the first place.

The part of a personal record that nothing cryptographically signs is often the part that matters most. Museum exhibitions, a server-free creator tool, and a wearable-camera company's IPO filing all tested, from different directions, what actually survives when a personal record leaves its creator's hands — and found that the signed, verifiable layer (the raw photo itself) is now table stakes that companies already give away for free, while the layer that often matters more (the structured record of what happened, and its own arrangement) typically carries no verification at all. None of the underlying investigations made this connection on their own; it only becomes visible once the week is read as a whole.
