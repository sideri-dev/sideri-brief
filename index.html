1. Japan's own Defense Ministry says a heritage label won't save a building from demolition — and names 820 of them.

A ministry survey found at least 820 former Imperial Japanese Army buildings still standing on Self-Defense Forces bases across 33 prefectures — aircraft plants, hangars, even former poison-gas and explosives factories, all built by 1945. The ministry is weighing demolition on age and seismic grounds, and says outright that some of the buildings already carry Japan Heritage status and that won't change the decision. It's a recurring pattern worth watching: a cultural label with no legal brake attached, stated this time by the demolishing party itself, at national scale.

2. A new retrieval method finds a matching image without ever being retrained on your database.

Patchify, presented at WACV 2026, does instance-level image retrieval by splitting each database image into a handful of structured patches and comparing those local features against a single query descriptor — no fine-tuning required as the collection grows. That matters for any growing, unlabeled image archive: new material needs to find its place in an existing index without the system being retrained every time something is added.

3. A working artist is treating his own 25-year archive as the exhibition, not the footnote to it.

Yuri Suzuki's London show "Onomatopia" pairs new fairground-ride sound sculptures with reference material straight out of his own practice — a 2001 Disney World anniversary map, a 2011 map an art collective made during his residency in Margate — displayed alongside the new prototypes rather than tucked into a catalogue essay. It's a clear case of a creator curating what he kept, not what an institution decided was worth keeping, which is exactly the kind of in-between material worth preserving automatically rather than relying on an artist to have saved it by hand.

4. A design icon's reissue rests on exactly one surviving photograph.

GLOCAL STANDARD PRODUCTS has relaunched Taro Okamoto's 1957 cube-form rattan chair, five years after the previous run ended when its maker lost its production site in the pandemic. The only archival image anyone cites to justify the faithful reissue is a single foundation-held photo of Okamoto with the chair in his studio. A small, concrete number — one photograph is what "documentation" meant for a mid-century design object at a real institution.

5. Thai lawmakers are telling the Fine Arts Department to document flood damage before it compounds.

A parliamentary committee is pushing Thailand's Fine Arts Department to survey two registered historic temples in Ayutthaya and Phetchaburi after flooding and set conservation-grade drainage guidelines, rather than just pumping the water out and moving on. A live, dated instance of a wider question across Southeast Asia's heritage sites: who documents damage, and when.

Yesterday's deep dive

Dug into the German state government's €3.25 million settlement that bought a regional museum usage and publication rights over the Joseph Beuys works it already physically holds, and found the actual contract terms are nowhere public: exclusivity, duration past 2056, and whether the museum's 200,000-plus archive items are even covered all go unstated. German law already lets a non-commercial museum digitise and index what it holds in-copyright, but confines access to on-site terminals — so the only boundary the state actually paid to cross was publication, not capture. The pattern is real (about a fifth of Europeana's 63 million records need rights-holder clearance before they can be shown), but this transaction is a sample of one, and museums are the institution type least affected by it. Japan draws a comparable line in soft-law form at 32,400 pixels for published derivatives of works a museum doesn't own outright; its new "unmanaged copyright" tribunal system, live since April, only reaches published works and lets a rights holder opt out by posting a usage rule, so it covers neither a Beuys-style estate dispute nor an artist's unpublished process material.
