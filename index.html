1. A German state just paid €3.25 million to learn that owning an archive isn't the same as owning the right to show it.

North Rhine-Westphalia has settled a years-long dispute with the Joseph Beuys Estate by funding a one-time €3.25m grant to Museum Schloss Moyland — not to buy the works, which the museum already holds (it's home to the largest Beuys collection anywhere), but to buy usage and publication rights over them. Copyright itself stays with the estate; a second outlet independently confirms the figure and notes earlier fights over exhibition and photography rights reached Germany's federal supreme court. It's as clean a real-world test as exists of the gap between custody and control — holding a record isn't the same as having the right to show it, which bears directly on any archiving effort built on material it doesn't itself own. The item is nearly eight months old with no stated reason for resurfacing, but a closed legal settlement doesn't go stale the way news does.

2. Tokyo just handed four buildings a historic label that comes with no actual protection.

Tokyo's metropolitan government selected two public bathhouses and two other buildings this month as "Tokyo Selected Historic Buildings." Unlike Japan's national designation tiers, this label carries zero legal restriction on altering or demolishing the buildings — the only ask is that anyone building within 100 metres be mindful of the historic landscape. It's a textbook instance of a regulatory gap worth watching: a building can carry a historic label and still have nothing standing between it and a wrecking ball.

3. A new paper teaches an algorithm to pick its own training tricks — and it works best exactly on sketches.

A method called ART treats the choice of image augmentations for self-supervised learning as a bandit problem, letting the system adaptively learn which transformations help rather than applying them at random. Tested on hand-drawn manatee sketches used for conservation tracking — about as texture-poor as images get — it beat both a random-augmentation baseline and frozen CLIP, and the paper names archaeological illustration as the same class of problem. That matters for connecting sketch-like, in-process imagery by visual similarity rather than by labels — most creative work-in-progress isn't a clean photograph.

4. UCLA just put a number on what institutional archive-preservation money actually looks like: roughly $100,000 a project, five times a year.

UCLA Library's Modern Endangered Archives Program funded its eighth cohort — 30 projects across 21 countries, from a record 250 applications — and just added a $2 million Arcadia-funded grant that, from 2027, pays for about five dedicated audio/video/film preservation awards a year, each up to $100,000. A rare, concrete figure for what institutional money actually pays for this kind of work.

5. One person spent five months manually fixing a museum's missing attributions — on just 700 objects.

A V&A cataloguing intern spent five months tracking down and re-attaching the correct names to roughly 700 drawings, paintings and prints by illustrator Randolph Caldecott, because half of them had no name attached in the museum's own collections database. A small, dated, very real labor number — one specialist, five months, 700 objects, no visual-similarity tooling anywhere in the process.

Yesterday's deep dive

Dug into Historic England's £1.16m SaaS digital preservation tender and found no award actually exists yet — four procurement releases, no contract, status still "active." The £1,160,000 figure is, by statute, the eight-year maximum across every renewal option, not a spend estimate — about £145,000 a year, 5.27 times what Historic England published at market engagement ten weeks earlier, for a 100TB archive that's run on the same asset manager since at least 2019. A real market price for this category does exist (Preservica's G-Cloud list, £13,980/year), and six comparable local-authority awards sit within about ±25% of it; the standard custody answer in that listing is 30 days after contract termination, after which the vendor may delete the client's data. OAIS, the preservation standard the tender is built on, places the duty to survive "the demise of the Archive" on the institution itself and says nothing about a third party holding the bits. In Japan, the equivalent national archive system is still at requirements stage on a contract running to March 2027, and the standard this UK tender leans on has no Japanese equivalent in the three primary policy documents checked.
