A major European design museum just opened a retrospective built almost entirely from an architect's own sketches, models and personal photographs — and it's already booked to tour Hong Kong.

The Vitra Design Museum's "Geoffrey Bawa: Architecture for the Senses" runs through February 2027, showing over 250 pieces — drawings, models, furniture, film and Bawa's own photographs — built in partnership with Hong Kong's M+, which the show travels to from June 2027. It's a clean, independently corroborated case of a museum choosing a practitioner's own process records, not just finished work, as the main exhibit.

OpenCV 5 quietly closed most of the gap between what it can run and what AI models people actually export — and the GPU work is now underway, not just promised.

The new DNN engine, accelerated on Arm chips via KleidiCV, lifts OpenCV's ONNX operator coverage from roughly 22% to over 80%. One correction worth carrying: some coverage of this release framed GPU acceleration as "planned for later releases" — the source's actual words are that GPU work "has started," not merely planned. It's still CPU-only today.

A CHI 2026 paper puts a hard number on how often hand-tracking cameras simply can't see your fingers — over one frame in five.

The paper, presented at the ACM CHI 2026 conference, finds "more than 20% of frames across all evaluated datasets exhibit at least one occluded finger" in egocentric hand-tracking data, and its method cuts tracking error 18% in the worst occlusion cases. It's XR gesture-sensing research rather than a passive-capture study, so its relevance to occlusion-robust capture devices generally is suggestive, not proven. Single source for now.

Yesterday's deep-dive

An earlier deep-dive found that the previous day's lead story about Singapore's privacy stance was reading from the least applicable part of the law: the "public space" exception the ministry cited doesn't extend to private premises like a studio, and a professional working in the course of business stays inside full consent obligations regardless of setting. The record also runs further than reported — five parliamentary answers exist through October 6, not two — and the real exposure sits in three places nobody had flagged: a security-equipment licensing law whose carve-out for self-installed systems is untested for a non-security creative instrument, breach-of-confidence case law that presumes a violation and shifts the burden of proof, and basic uncertainty over whether Singapore's privacy act reaches a seller domiciled elsewhere in Asia at all. Capture indicators — a light or sound signaling that a device is recording — were asked about twice in parliament and answered neither time. This is not legal advice, and several of the central questions are genuinely unresolved rather than settled either way.
