Today

Texas's new right-to-repair law, HB 2963, took effect September 1 — and it explicitly keeps countertop appliances in scope even as it exempts major appliances.

The law covers anything with embedded digital electronics sold at $50 or more wholesale. It exempts refrigerators, ovens, microwaves and heating/cooling units — but says so "excluding other countertop or stand-alone small appliances," meaning those stay covered. Covered makers must offer parts, tools and documentation on fair terms within a year of first sale, though source code and anti-theft measures stay protected. It's a real test case for how repair law is starting to treat premium kitchen hardware specifically, rather than lumping it in with the major-appliance categories that keep getting carved out.

GoPro drew the final $20 million of its Yorkville debt facility, according to its own SEC filing.

The draw closes out a $50 million facility, matures next August, and caps conversion so no single holder can end up owning more than 4.99% of the stock — dilutive financing layered on top of a $285 million merger already underway. It's another entry in a recognizable pattern for a struggling hardware brand: floor-priced convertible debt running in parallel with a control-changing deal.

Anker is folding five brands — eufy, Soundcore and others — into a single name, and just opened its first standalone store, in Berlin.

The company's CEO put it plainly: "Category brands made sense when categories were separate. They no longer are." The Berlin store opened September 5. It's a useful counter-case for anyone weighing owned physical retail against staying purely online — a much larger consumer-electronics player is betting on brand-concentrated, in-person retail right now.

Yesterday's deep-dive

An investigation into the EU's Cyber Resilience Act found that, two days before its vulnerability-reporting rule takes effect, the actual operating rulebook is a web page rather than the regulation's own legal text, which does not yet exist in final form. The real cost of compliance turns out to be procedural: one named individual needs a personal, verified account before any backup contact can even be added — a single point of failure by design. The launch version of the reporting platform is thinner than advertised too, with web-form-only submission, no proper API, and at least one required field the platform doesn't yet actually contain, which is the documented cause of a known bug in the regulator's own reporting-deadline countdown. Separately, the regulator's own guidance documents disagree with each other about whether a finished-device maker or the maker of an underlying chip component bears the reporting duty when a shared flaw is found — meaning liability for a bug in third-party firmware code would likely fall on whoever ships the finished product, not the chip vendor.

This week

A thermal-imaging sensor's real limits this week turned out to be physical, not algorithmic: frame rate is capped by a microcontroller's data bus well below the sensor's rated top speed, the sensor can't be put to sleep and costs several minutes of accuracy on every wake, and a machine-learning adaptation technique that looked promising on paper turns out to have never been tested on infrared data at all — its assumed failure modes, like steam and condensation, are optical and mechanical problems fixable with a fan or a shield, not software.

Every automated channel that should surface a bug in a chip vendor's own shipped firmware reported nothing this week — a public vulnerability feed missed an entire batch of fixes, and the one real defect that did surface had no formal vulnerability number at all, appearing only on the chip vendor's own permanent errata page. Separately, EU rules would put the reporting duty for a bug in someone else's underlying code on the device maker, not the component maker — a cost-of-entry detail for markets not yet being entered.

Several deep-dives this week ended up correcting the very news item that triggered them, and each correction was the same shape: the quote was accurate, but the conclusion about what it actually meant was wrong — a platform feature turned out to have eligibility requirements that excluded the reader, a "slipping" restock date turned out to be a computed estimate rather than real supply news. Checking a quote is not the same as checking what it implies.
