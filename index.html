A research preprint compresses long workplace video into a structured, provenance-linked "Procedural State Memory."

The framework, from researchers Vivek Chavan and Jörg Krüger, segments continuous first-person and third-person video into evidence-linked event cards — actor, time interval, location, action, objects, before/after state, confidence, provenance — running on frozen DINOv2 and V-JEPA-2 vision encoders plus a local language model, explicitly scoped to on-premise privacy constraints. No benchmark numbers are reported yet; this is architecture, not a proven result. It's a close match to the broader idea of turning passive video into a structured, locally retrievable record.

A major design museum put 600 objects in a room with no chronology, no geography, and no hierarchy — on purpose.

Munich's Die Neue Sammlung opened X-D-E-P-O-T, a new permanent exhibition spanning 30 design themes across 600 objects, organized explicitly against curatorial ranking. The museum's own words: "no chronology, no geographical boundaries and no hierarchy." It's institutional validation, from a design museum rather than a software company, of browsing logic that answers without ranking or suggesting.

A Tokyo museum turned archaeological excavation scan data into the exhibit itself.

Opening October 8, "THE FIRST RAILWAY — Hajimari no Tetsudō" at MoN Takanawa: The Museum of Narratives presents a video work, "A Locomotive Running on the Sea," built directly from point-cloud scan data captured during the archaeological dig at the site of Japan's first railway embankment (1872), narrated by actor Matsushige Yutaka with materials supplied by JR East. Passive capture data gathered years ago, during someone else's working process, became the exhibited artifact — on view in Tokyo now.

The V&A's second "Craft x Tech" pairs six international designers with named Japanese regional artisan workshops, no fixed brief.

"Evolving Craft," curated by Maria Cristina Didero with Tangent's Hideki Yoshimoto, sets designers including Bethan Laura Wood and Philippe Malouin loose on open-ended "four hands" collaborations with Tokai-region ateliers — Arimatsu Narumi Shibori, Iga Kumihimo, and Mino Washi among them. It runs through October 18 in the V&A's Daylit Gallery in London, the second edition of a curatorial pipeline connecting outside designers directly to Japanese craft manufacturing.
