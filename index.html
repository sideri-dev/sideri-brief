Espressif's ESP-IDF v6.1-rc1 locks in a set of driver-breaking changes ahead of the stable release.

The release candidate moves flash-driver internals behind private headers (code should call the API, not touch the struct directly), changes the storage format for mbedTLS's PSA persistent keys so they need re-importing, temporarily disables ECDSA Secure Boot V2 on the H2/C5/P4 chip line pending a fix, and drops support for shared interrupt flags in SPI initialization. It's the kind of pre-1.0 API churn worth tracking closely for anyone building new firmware against the v6.x line's non-legacy driver APIs.

Sharp is showing its own design process in public for the first time in roughly 30 years.

"Sharp Design's Gaze Exhibition" runs free through August 28 at GOOD DESIGN Marunouchi in Tokyo: five working products — a quantum-dot miniLED TV, a drum washer, a robot vacuum, a water heater, a stick vacuum — shown alongside prototypes and internal development documents, staged at the actual vantage points Sharp's designers used to observe the products in use. It follows a September 2025 design-philosophy relaunch and a new design center that opened in December. A major appliance maker publicly staging "how we watched the thing get used" as the exhibition's own subject is a rare and useful precedent for framing sensor-driven observation as design language rather than surveillance.

A tiny AI model listens for an endangered bumblebee in Patagonia — and runs on 794 microjoules per classification.

MIT Media Lab, working with Kioxia, built a 158,144-parameter INT8-quantized CNN running on a low-power microcontroller with a built-in neural accelerator (Analog Devices' MAX78000), hitting 86.1% three-class accuracy at 10.4 milliseconds per audio segment, purpose-built for unattended field deployment. It's a genuinely useful reference point for anyone sizing a small quantized classifier meant to triage a continuous sensor stream into discrete events entirely on-device, at real microcontroller power budgets rather than cloud-inference assumptions.

A single chip just detected six gases down to parts-per-billion levels on a 3.5-by-4.5-centimeter board.

Researchers at Jinan University, Shanxi University, and the Chinese Academy of Sciences built an on-chip lithium niobate platform combining photoacoustic and thermoelastic spectroscopy with photodetection, co-packaged with a mid-infrared laser: ammonia at roughly 7 parts per billion, nitrogen dioxide at about 2ppb, acetylene at about 3.5ppb, methane at about 2.5ppb, plus carbon dioxide and water vapor, spanning visible to mid-infrared light. It's a meaningfully different gas-sensing architecture from today's typical metal-oxide resistive sensors — parts-per-billion sensitivity, multiple gases, all on one chip. So far the result is published in one peer-reviewed study only, so treat it as an early signal rather than a settled fact.

Teenage Engineering just got its first official distributor in Thailand.

RTB Technology launched as the authorized importer for Teenage Engineering's Field System, EP Series, and Pocket Operator lines, selling through its own headquarters and Music Arms in Bangkok, with prices running from 3,290 baht up to 99,000 baht for the OP-XY (the OP-1 Field at 88,500 baht). It's a live test of how a Southeast Asian market receives premium, instrument-grade hardware sold at those price points through an official local channel — a useful signal for anyone weighing how "instrument, not gadget" positioning travels outside its home markets.

Yesterday's deep-dive — smart-kitchen graveyard and survivors

The 2015–2021 smart-kitchen hardware generation is now largely dead: one CES-era smart oven's cloud services shut off next month, another company backed by a major commercial-kitchen-equipment maker ceased operations in March, and a well-known smart-cooktop brand's app retired last July — all three tracing to the same pattern, cloud-dependent features with no matching recurring revenue and no disclosed support window. The survivors split into two camps: hardware that works fully offline with optional subscriptions, or hardware subsidized by ongoing consumables and content revenue (one direct-sales kitchen-appliance maker just posted a record €2.1 billion year in its culinary division). The newest wave of entrants is dominated by funded camera-vision robotic cookers, while thermal-imaging-first competitors remain underfunded or unverified — and notably, nothing on the market yet pairs true thermal imaging with gas or VOC sensing in one cooking-focused device. Category venture funding collapsed from roughly $169 million in 2021 to about $44 million in 2025. The clearest lesson: any device in this category needs a graceful offline mode and an answer for who pays for the cloud in year four, decided before the first unit ships — because feature advantages here commoditize faster than a hardware company can recoup them.
