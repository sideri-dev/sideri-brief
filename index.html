Raspberry Pi raised prices again on the board family our capture hardware is built on.

The company's own blog confirms a $12.50 rise on the 2GB Raspberry Pi 4 and Pi 5, to $67.50 and $77.50, citing memory costs that "have risen very steeply over the past two years and continue to increase." The 1GB variants and the Compute Module 4/5 2GB tier are explicitly spared — this is a memory-cost move, not a general repricing, and two independent outlets corroborate the figures the same day.

It extends a pattern already logged against HP, Lenovo and Framework onto this specific platform vendor: the DRAM shortage isn't abstract, it's landing on the exact board this project ships on. We declined to carry a researcher's "third hike since launch" framing — the vendor's own post doesn't make that claim, so it goes in flagged as an unverified lead rather than fact.

Polaroid's new instant camera puts its creative controls on physical dials, and stages its launch in exactly the sequence we've been debating for our own.

Mod ships today direct from Polaroid.com at $169.99, reaches selected US retail October 6, and goes global "from early 2027" — a clean, dated precedent for staging a direct-to-consumer launch ahead of retail and international rollout. The chief product officer's framing lands close to home too: "we wanted the creative experience to be built into the physical design of the camera... That's why the four shooting modes sit directly on the dial rather than behind menus or settings."

Single-sourced today — Polaroid's own site returned a rate-limit error when we tried to corroborate directly — but the reporting is solid and dated.

A competitor's chief designer just walked through the exact battery trade-off our own hardware has to make.

Strutt's ev¹ powered wheelchair chose LFP cells over higher energy density for safety and longevity, accepting a physically larger pack as the cost — the same safety-over-density logic behind our own user-replaceable, locally-sourceable battery approach. The piece is unusually candid about iteration: drop tests cracked the carrying handle, so the team softened the corner radius and changed the internal structure; the button and LEDs were later cut entirely "for better waterproofing and reduced complexity."

Worth reading end to end as a template for how to talk publicly about a battery decision once a device built this way ships.

New research finds an AI "organizing" an infinite canvas doesn't stay neutral — it starts curating, whether it was meant to or not.

A CHI conference field study tracked eight professional designers across 5,838 design actions and found an AI organizing agent's role shifts "from a divergent catalyst in early stages to a convergent curator in later phases," pulling cognitive effort away from spatial management and into content curation.

That's a direct empirical challenge to the idea that a retrieval tool can stay neutral and never curate — not a theoretical worry, a measured behavioral drift in the identical canvas-based interaction model we're building around. Single academic study, not yet replicated, but exactly the kind of evidence that should prompt a hard look at how firm that boundary actually holds under real use.

Someone already open-sourced a cheap reference design for the reliability problem this kind of hardware still has to solve.

Ego-OSCAR pairs an embedded Linux board handling on-device video encoding with a separate real-time microcontroller handling watchdog and user-feedback duties — the same board-plus-watchdog split an always-on capture device needs for unattended reliability — at a stated bill-of-materials under $200 using off-the-shelf parts and 3D-printed components. The team has already logged roughly 550 hours of synchronized stereo-video-plus-motion-sensor capture on it.

Worth a close read as a concrete cost and architecture anchor, even though it's a single preprint with no independent replication yet.
