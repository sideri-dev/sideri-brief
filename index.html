Today

Raspberry Pi's own blog confirms its PC/Mac desktop build now runs on 64-bit Debian Trixie.

The release moves from the long-dead 32-bit x86 image to 64-bit Debian, folds in the Raspberry Pi Connect remote-access client, and adds a new Control Centre and dock. Apple Silicon support stays experimental, so this is still a PC/Intel-Mac release rather than a Pi-everywhere one. It settles an open question about exactly which Debian release line this platform now stands on — a detail that matters for anyone building firmware on top of it, including our own prototype's Pi 5 build.

A Japanese eyewear chain is about to crowdfund a ¥119,000 pair of AI glasses through its own national retail network.

MeganeTop's "Linse Display" glasses carry a 12-megapixel camera and an in-view AI display, weigh about 50 grams, and run a 200mAh battery. Crowdfunding opens October 23 on GREEN FUNDING, after which the product goes to nationwide sale through MeganeTop's own 眼鏡市場 eyewear stores — confirmed independently by two Japanese outlets reporting the price, date and retail sequence the same way. It's a live, dated data point for what a premium camera-equipped wearable can charge in Japan, and for crowdfund-then-retail as a real launch pattern rather than a theory.

Kanazawa's National Crafts Museum is staging a 100-year retrospective on how craft became design.

"Design Chronicle" (October 9, 2026 – January 11, 2027) runs four chapters — Arts & Crafts and Art Nouveau, Art Deco, Modernist Design, and the postwar period — built on the museum's own argument that today's idea of "design" emerged from craftspeople's trial-and-error under industrialization. Enzo Mari, Marianne Brandt, Marcel Breuer and Tom Dixon are on the checklist. It's a strong institutional vote for the view that craft and design were never opposites — craft is where design came from.

A CVPR 2026 paper puts a hard number on "mostly grayscale, color only when it matters" for always-on cameras.

ColorTrigger holds 91.6% of a full-color model's performance while actually capturing full-color frames only 8.1% of the time — training-free, aimed squarely at power- and compute-constrained edge devices that need to watch continuously. It's a single preprint, not yet independently replicated, but it gives a concrete power/compute budget for an always-on triggering approach: run a cheap grayscale stream continuously, and reserve full-color capture for the small fraction of frames that actually warrant it.

San Francisco's Museum of Craft and Design has reopened "OBJECTS: USA," the 1969 show that founded the American studio-craft movement's premium argument.

The restaging, running through January 17, 2027, brings back more than 70 works from the original 300-plus-artist show — Wendell Castle, George Nakashima, Kay Sekimachi, Toshiko Takaezu and Art Smith among them — organized in partnership with R & Company. The museum frames craft, in its own words, as "a space for freedom, experimentation, and artistic possibility." Same thread as the Kanazawa show above: a primary institutional source making the case that craft's value sits in process and experimentation, not in finish.

Yesterday's deep-dive

An earlier deep-dive found that a Raspberry Pi blog post on a third-party AI accelerator board was weaker evidence than it first looked: the body of the post was written by the accelerator vendors themselves as a guest contribution, not by Raspberry Pi independently, so its own best-case benchmark numbers turn out to run about 119 times faster than the real-world figure the same vendor publishes elsewhere. The genuinely useful finding survives, though: on the primaries that actually matter for an edge-AI accelerator decision, the heavy compute still runs on the host CPU rather than the dedicated chip, so a compiled model file alone doesn't prove the accelerator is doing the work — nobody has yet published a real-world benchmark for this class of vision-embedding model on this chip. One genuine positive held up: a small image-embedding output fits comfortably inside this chip's efficient operating range, and a competing accelerator vendor's own published accuracy-versus-speed tradeoff is the best real comparison point available anywhere.
