Today

Oura just pulled its $2.2 billion Nasdaq IPO, and the market is the reason given, not the company.

Oura had marketed 50 million shares at $40–44 apiece, implying a $15.62 billion valuation, before postponing days ahead of pricing — a spike in bond yields, fears of more Federal Reserve rate hikes, and geopolitical turmoil all cited. Mergermarket's Samuel Kerr: "even strong businesses with spectacular economics will face greater scrutiny." It extends an S-1 disclosure from a couple of weeks ago (85% twelve-month paid-member retention) with the outcome: public markets are now repricing hardware-plus-subscription valuations downward — relevant to how we think about subscription models on our own hardware.

A new nacre-inspired composite does two jobs our always-on hardware needs, in one material layer.

Published in Nature Communications, it delivers passive cooling of up to 20.5 K with no fan, dissipates 97.9% of kinetic impact force (shrugging off a 50 m/s projectile hit), and keeps its performance through closed-loop recycling. For an unattended device that has to survive drops and run fanless without compromising a repairable, recyclable build, this is a concrete candidate worth tracking — single-source and dated to June, but nothing like it is elsewhere in our notes.

A sub-1-billion-parameter model now does what used to need many billions: one embedding space for text, speech, image and video together.

Omni-Embed-Mini, just posted to arXiv, binds all those modalities into a shared space without degrading text retrieval, at 2.7–9.5x fewer parameters than existing omni-modal embedders. Our own on-device, offline-only search architecture needs exactly this — a lightweight way to search a personal image archive by text query — so this is a real candidate, though single-source and worth re-checking before it goes any further.

A London designer just built the clearest precedent yet for "the record is the object."

Kiran Scott de Martinville's "Sky Vase," shown at London Design Festival, uses a custom anodising rig to turn the sky at a specific place and date, chosen by its owner, into a permanent titanium colour gradient — literally encoding a capture event into the material itself. It's a sharp outside validation of the instinct behind our own passive-capture-into-an-owned-record thesis.

Yesterday's deep-dive

Yesterday's target — the A-POC ABLE ISSEY MIYAKE × JAXA show — turned out to be a correction job, not a confirmation. The exhibition hasn't opened yet (it starts October 21, not "now," as first reported), and its two cited sources turned out to share identical organizer copy rather than being independent corroboration. What the deeper look found instead is more useful: the gallery slot is a leasable, rate-carded launch space with retail rights built in, and the garment encodes its own cutting pattern and a satellite image pre-woven into the cloth itself — the record built into the substrate, not printed on it. Tested against our own framework for telling a captured working process apart from a finished artefact, this sits on the artefact side, not the process-record side; the one case in the whole sweep that does the latter is an artist's process film and dataset held in a major museum's permanent collection.
