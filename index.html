MIT and Sumitomo built an excavator controller where novices perform like experts "from the start."

Instead of a joystick operators have to learn to map onto bucket motion, MIT's Hermano Krebs lab and Sumitomo Heavy Industries built a miniature mechanical arm operators physically grasp, with motion mirrored on a six-screen virtual display — removing the mental-mapping step entirely. In a seven-day training study, joystick-trained novices stayed consistently worse than experts throughout, while operators trained on this interface matched expert performance immediately. Two independent outlets, MIT News and Core77, confirm the same figures and quotes. It's a strong precedent for a thesis worth taking seriously in any interface-heavy hardware design: a well-built interface, not machine learning, is what closes a skill gap between novice and expert users.

Meridian Innovation unveiled a new thermal sensor with over three times the pixel count of a widely used comparable, in a similar package.

The Cheetah packs a 50×50 array (2,500 pixels, versus 768 on Melexis' MLX90640) into a 7×7×7mm module on Meridian's existing SenXor platform, with mass production slated for early 2026 and "smart appliances and IoT" named as a target application. Meridian already has a foothold in thermal-sensor comparisons via an earlier product, the SenXor MI0802 — this is a second line from the same vendor worth tracking. No published price or noise-equivalent temperature figure yet, so it's a watch rather than something to spec against today.

SwitchBot's new ¥19,980 wearable AI recorder routes everything through a cloud LLM the user picks.

The 16.8g clip records up to 20 hours continuously with 64GB onboard storage, launched August 19, and lets the wearer choose which cloud model — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini — summarizes what it hears, confirmed independently by GIGAZINE. It's a clean real-world example of one architectural path for turning raw sensor data into meaning: routing it through a cloud LLM at inference time, rather than running lighter-weight pattern comparison locally on the device.

Fairphone's newly launched repairable phone ships with a free screwdriver and a full spare-parts price list — but Fairphone's own store and the press coverage of it don't agree on the prices.

Core77 lists a battery at $46, display at $104, and main camera at $81, alongside a bundled T5 Torx screwdriver; Fairphone's own US store, fetched directly today, prices the identical parts lower — $39.95, $89.95, $69.95. Both sources are dated today and credible; the discrepancy is unresolved rather than silently picked one way. Either way it's a costed, launch-day example of what a spare-parts and repair-tools program can look like for a user-repairable device.

Yesterday's deep-dive — shipping hardware internationally from Japan

Shipping a premium instrument-class device internationally splits into three regimes depending entirely on the installed battery: postal-cheap (Singapore, roughly 4% of retail value to land a parcel, zero duty), postal-encumbered (the US, where the postal lane keeps closing and reopening under shifting customs rules), and postal-impossible (Germany, the UK, Thailand — no lithium-air-mail path exists, forcing a pricier contract courier). A widely cited 15% US duty figure turned out to be a stale screenshot of a rate the Supreme Court had already struck down; the actual current rate is a flat 12.5% under the operative tariff action in force since July. Consumer returns can't physically travel back to Japan by mail in most cases — the US bans it outright for used lithium devices — so returns economics need a destination-side hub or a reserve fund regardless of market. Singapore comes out as the cheapest and simplest lane of every market studied, while Thailand is the structurally hardest despite mattering strategically.
