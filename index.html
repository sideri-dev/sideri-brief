1. Japan's own Ministry of Finance just audited the heritage-restoration subsidy system, and crowdfunding barely moves the needle.

Auditing 124 subsidized restoration projects, the ministry found 65% of owners did no outside fundraising at all, and where owners did try donations, hometown-tax contributions or crowdfunding, that money covered only 3% of project cost on average. Not one of the surveyed owners used the subsidy's own matching-donation bonus — worth up to 20% extra — even though it's available. The base subsidy itself covers 50% of eligible cost with no upper limit, funded at roughly ¥15.8 billion this fiscal year. For anyone trying to work out who actually pays to keep a protected historic building standing in Japan, the government's own numbers say: the state does, overwhelmingly — not the owner, and not the crowd.

2. Singapore's planning authority is quietly making it cheaper for owners to maintain conserved buildings.

The Urban Redevelopment Authority is reviewing its rules for conserved buildings so owners can carry out routine upkeep with less paperwork, and is studying whether some low-impact work can skip the application process altogether. No list of exempted work or start date has been published — this is a stated intention, not yet a rule — but it's a live signal that the ongoing cost of keeping a heritage building maintained, not just the cost of documenting it once, is heading down.

3. A building's own archived drawings, with nothing else, were enough to rebuild it in 3D and show exactly where it diverged from the original plan.

Researchers reconstructed Gio Ponti's unused 1950s Garzanti Foundation building in Forlì, Italy, purely from drawings held in a university archive — no laser scan of the real structure, no photogrammetry, just the paper record. It's close to a working proof that a well-kept drawing archive carries enough design intent to preserve a building's original form even after it's altered or lost, and to check later work against it.

4. The world's biggest paper-records storage company is growing fastest somewhere other than storing paper records.

Iron Mountain's data-center segment grew 34% year-over-year in the third quarter of 2026, nearly three times the 11% organic growth in its legacy storage-rental business — a segment-level split not previously public in this detail. The company whose whole historical business model is "keep your records for a recurring fee" is now growing faster in cloud and data infrastructure than in the records-custody business itself.

Yesterday's deep dive

A new technique called VIBRA lets a freshly retrained image-search encoder query an old, already-indexed photo collection without re-processing it — and it genuinely works, winning all 50 of its published test settings. But the same paper's own data shows that gain is only 55% of what simply re-encoding everything from scratch would deliver, and the point where this kind of compatibility training becomes cheaper than a full re-encode sits above a million images — well beyond the size of most working photo archives, where re-encoding stays the cheap option anyway. Every one of the paper's 50 tests also matches text against images, not one photo against another, which is the actual shape of the problem for an archive browsed by visual similarity. Our take, unchanged: version every embedding, never mix versions in one index, and re-process fully whenever the underlying model changes — on-device re-encoding is cheap enough at this scale that there's little reason to do otherwise.
